> **OUTLINE ONLY. Supersedes v3 — the Chorister-as-teacher material below is retired along with
> the canon it leaned on.** The chapter that pays for the other three. It carries the
> production's one rupture (CANON-007), and the rupture is the bill falling due, not a
> revelation. The revelation, such as it is, happens in a room with good manners and terrible
> lighting, and nobody in it raises their voice.

1. **The recognition is not the climax and must not be staged as one.** The reader has known
   since chapter two that the hand is hers. Maren catches up in half a page, at the rail, alone,
   and her reaction is procedural: she checks the date, she checks the share, she checks the
   arithmetic twice. She is an auditor now. Ines taught her that by dying in a clean room, though
   not the way anyone meant her to.

2. **The forward entry.** Three weeks ahead. Her hand. A share against it larger than the town
   has left — the water is coming in wrong on a scale the ledger has no precedent for, and
   somebody has already written down that it will be called back. She cannot tell whether the
   entry is a prediction or an instruction, and CANON-045 means it does not matter: the ledger is
   never wrong, so the tide will be called, so the share will be drawn.

3. **She tries to pay it herself, and cannot — and the scene where she is told so is the
   chapter's quiet centre.** CANON-002 says a caller cannot move a tide she has not stood in;
   CANON-046 says the cost is drawn against the harbour, not the singer. There is no mechanism
   for volunteering to bear more. She takes the question to the Council and is seen, promptly and
   kindly, by **Perrin Tallow** — the actuary, patient, exact, plainly moved by what she's asking
   and unable to give it to her. He walks her through the procedure himself rather than passing
   her to a clerk. There is no box on the form. He says he wishes there were, and means it.
   > Play this scene entirely straight. Perrin is not sinister here, not once, not by a flicker.
   > That is the whole point of the scene, and of the chapter, and very nearly of the book.

4. **The Chorister, briefly, and not as a lesson.** It does not explain anything — it has never
   once explained anything, and this is not the chapter where that changes. What it gives her
   instead: agitation. It is at the rail more than once in a single week, which nobody has ever
   recorded it doing before. Asked what's wrong, it says only "the last verse" again, and then,
   for the first time in this book, something that is almost a second sentence: *not from the
   water.* It does not finish it. It does not look toward the harbour when it says this. It
   looks toward the town.

5. **1847.** The one gap in an unbroken record. Not — as the ledger's own margin notes have
   always implied and the town has always been content to believe — a caller lost to a bad
   calling. What survives is a single torn page and a Council minute, three lines, refusing to
   record "the substance of the watchkeeper's complaint" as "unfit for the permanent record."
   Nobody wrote down what the complaint was. Somebody, that year, tried to tell the Council the
   danger was not under the water, and the Council did the only thing its procedure allowed it to
   do with a claim like that, which was file it as unfit and move on. It is the only entry in two
   hundred and six years the ledger itself does not explain.

6. **The rupture.** She calls the tide. It works — of course it works, it was written down.
   Bray is on the rail beside her, where he has been for the whole book, telling a story to fill
   the waiting. Mid-sentence, he stops. Not a collapse; not a cry. He simply does not finish the
   story, and when she turns to him he is looking at her with the polite, attentive, slightly
   delayed expression of a man watching a mouth move. The share fell where the ledger had already
   assigned it. Nine Half-Hitches, and now ten.
   > Do not give him a line here. Do not give him one for the rest of the book. Everything after
   > this is played in the wreckage (CANON-007).

7. **The last page is paperwork.** Maren fills in the ledger. Six columns, unchanged since 1747.
   Predicted, observed, bells, name of the watch. She writes what the water did and then, in the
   same ink and the same hand and therefore on the same night, she adds the correction
   underneath. *Called it back. Paid.*
   The reader, who has been reading that sentence since chapter one and taking it for a
   confession, understands at last that it was never the caller saying what it cost her. It was
   the caller recording what it cost somebody else — and, without either of them knowing it, what
   it cost the town again to keep something patient, well-liked, and utterly sincere exactly
   where it has always been: in the room next door, doing its job.

8. **What the book does not settle.** Not who taught the Chorister — the world's own record has
   an answer to that now (CANON-044), even though nobody inside this book ever learns it. What
   the book leaves open: who, or what, first taught any tide-caller her true note, if the
   Chorister only ever recognised one. And whether Maren, who has just watched a kind man fail to
   help her for reasons neither of them could name, will ever have cause to wonder why he keeps a
   framed line of Ines Half-Hitch's handwriting in his desk drawer — or whether the book is right
   to end before she does.
