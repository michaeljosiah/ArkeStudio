The ledger of the Vigil is kept in a hand that changes every generation and a form that never
has: date, moon, tide predicted, tide observed, bells, name of the watch. Six columns since
1747. The Council has tried twice to modernise it and been talked out of it both times by
people who could not say why it mattered, only that it did.

Maren has the 1820 volume open on the rail desk because the binding is failing and somebody has
to say so in writing. She is not reading it. You do not read the ledger; you check it, the way
you check a lock.

The entry for the eleventh of March is ordinary. Neap tide predicted, neap tide observed, no
bells, watch signed *E. Kell* — a name that runs through the harbour like a seam and means
nothing in particular. Then the correction.

Somebody has gone back to the observed column, struck through *neap*, and written above it, in
the same ink and the same hand and therefore on the same night: **a hand-span high, and rising
when it should not have been.** Under it, smaller: *called it back. paid.*

Maren reads that twice. Then she reads it a third time, because on the third reading she notices
that the crossing-out is neat. Not the scrape of a mistake caught. The stroke of somebody who
had the correct answer in mind before they wrote the wrong one, and wrote it anyway, because the
form has a column for what the tables say and a column for what the water does and only a fool
confuses them.

She has made that same stroke. Tuesday. Two hundred and six years later, in a different volume,
in a different ink.
