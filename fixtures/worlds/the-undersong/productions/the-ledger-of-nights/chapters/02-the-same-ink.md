The 1901 volume is in better condition and worse order. Somebody rebound it in the fifties and
lost the March gathering doing it, which is the sort of thing that happens to records nobody
believes are important until the year they are.

What survives of the eighty-first year is a single night, and the night is wrong in a way Maren
cannot immediately name. She sits with it through slack water before it comes to her.

The predicted column is in one ink. The observed column is in the same ink. On every other page
in the volume they are different — you write the prediction at dusk from the tables and the
observation at slack water from the rail, and between those two acts a pen dries and gets
refilled and the colour moves, and a hundred and fifty years of pages show it moving.

Not here. Here somebody sat down once, at one hour, and filled in both columns together. And the
observed column is correct. The tide did what the second column said it would do, six hours
before it did it.

Maren has the archivist bring the 1820 volume back up and puts the two pages side by side under
the lamp. The hand is the same. Not similar — the harbour's schools taught one hand for two
centuries and half the ledger looks alike. The same. The same hesitation before a descender, the
same refusal to close the loop on a lowercase *g*.

There is one more thing on the 1901 page, and she nearly misses it because it is on every page:
a margin note, small, in the watch's own ordinary hand. *No dreams reported.* She flips back
through the volume and finds it again and again, decades apart — never every night, but always,
she begins to notice, within a night or two of the pages that are wrong. She takes it for watch
idiom, some old way of saying nothing stirred.

*Eighty-one years,* she says, to nobody, because the Vigil's rail is not manned by two people
between dusk and slack water and never has been.
