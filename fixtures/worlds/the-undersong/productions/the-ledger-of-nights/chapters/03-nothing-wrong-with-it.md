The 1974 audit runs to forty pages and finds nothing, and the forty pages are how you know how
badly it wanted to.

The Council had asked a simple question — is the ledger being falsified — and it is the nature
of simple questions that the answer arrives in a form nobody ordered. The ledger is not being
falsified. Every predicted tide matches the tables of its year. Every observed tide matches the
harbour records held independently at the weigh-house. The bells column correlates with nothing,
which is what the bells column has always done.

The auditor was a clerk called Ines Half-Hitch, and she did the work properly, and the work
properly done took her somewhere nobody had sent her. On page thirty-one she stops reconciling
tides and starts reconciling costs, because the ledger has a column for what was paid and she
is the first person in two hundred years to add it up.

The total does not fit inside the callers. It never has. It is roughly nine times what the
sixteen recorded tide-callers of the harbour could have spent between them if every one of them
had gone stone deaf on their last night, and half of them died hearing well enough to be
annoyed by the hurdy-gurdy.

So she goes to the north gate and reads the box.

It takes her an afternoon; the orthography is four centuries old and worn nearly through. What
it says is not a mystery, or a curse, or a warning. It is a term of agreement, and it is the
plainest sentence Ines has read all year. *For the keeping of the water, I will bear my share of
the keeping.*

She has nodded at that box since she was six.

The audit's recommendation is that the ledger be continued unchanged. It is correct. There is no
finding of falsification, because there was no falsification; there is no finding of harm,
because harm was not the question she was asked; and there is no mechanism in the Council's
procedure by which a clerk may report that a thing is functioning exactly as designed and that
this is the problem.

So she files it correctly, and adds one line beneath the recommendation in her own hand.

*The record is accurate. I would like it noted that I do not find this reassuring.*

The fair copy for the Council archive is made by her junior that same week — careful, a little
too careful, the kind of young clerk who copies a thing twice before he trusts it copied once.
He keeps a second copy of the last line for himself. He could not have said why, then or since.

Fifty-two years later Maren reads that line, and then, because she has begun to think like an
auditor, she goes and finds the other document — the ward register, kept in the same building,
two floors down, by a different office that has never had cause to compare notes.

Thirty-one names. Nine of them Half-Hitch. The ninth was admitted in 1981 and is the woman who
wrote the line.
