One verse a season, without exception, for as far back as the ledger runs. Whatever else was
uncertain about the Undersong, its pace was the one thing the harbour could set a calendar by.

That has stopped being true within living memory. There were two verses last winter. Three this
spring. And the most recent one did not describe the night it arrived on. It described a night
three weeks ahead, in enough particular detail — tide height, wind, who was on the rail — that
nobody has been able to argue it is a metaphor.

The verse has never before had anything to say about a night that had not happened. It corrected
the present; it did not describe the future. Something that only ever spoke in an eternal present
tense has started using a different one, and nobody who studies it can say whether the song has
started running ahead of the harbour, or whether the harbour is only now catching up to a pace
the song was always keeping.

Nobody knows what it is approaching. If it is a lament, it is close to being sung out. If it is a
labour, it is close to term. If it is a reading, it is close to the last page. In two hundred and
six years nobody has ever heard the Undersong end, because it has never, until now, sounded like
something that could.

**Amended at revision 63 (CANON-052).** Not an ending in the sense the town fears. A stitch
pulled tighter, faster, more often, is not a god approaching its final page — it is a wound that
has started costing more to hold shut than it used to, after two centuries of something on the
other side of it slowly finishing the work of becoming someone. Nobody has measured the Unkept
against a clock before, because there was never a reason to. There is now.

**Amended at revision 101 (CANON-069).** And the acceleration's simplicity has a colder
reading than decay: bandwidth. A mind that must pay for every syllable it sends across a
failing bridge stops sending sentences. More verses, plainer verses, a phrase where there used
to be a season of music — that is not necessarily a smaller intelligence. It may be a large one
economising, spending its narrowing budget on the only words that still matter. The Last Verse
may be the cheapest possible form of the most important thing it has ever had to say.
