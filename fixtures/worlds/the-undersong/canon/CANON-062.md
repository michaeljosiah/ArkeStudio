Where the margins allow them to be counted — the ledger's dreamless notations (CANON-058), the
Charter-era registers, two centuries of civic memory — the Unremembered's previous lives can be
partly assembled. The assembly is not what anyone expects.

Some were cruel. Some were heroic. One was a physician of rare gentleness. One was executed for
a murder they did not commit, and the record of the trial reads, now, like a town punishing a
wrongness it could feel and not name. One died saving dozens of children from a flooded
tenement, and has a small statue, and the statue's face has weathered faster than its stone
should allow.

And one — a Charter-era harbour clerk — worked out what they were. They spent the rest of a
short life trying to prevent their own return, failed, and left four lines in a hand the record
has seen elsewhere:

> I do not fear what follows me.
> I fear what follows as me.

**Every one of these lives was real.** Each personality genuine, each kindness meant, each
grief felt. There is no passenger behind the eyes, no demon wearing a person. The person *is*
the echo (CANON-054), and the horror of the awakening — if it comes — is not transformation but
recognition: Perrin would remain Perrin, loving what Perrin loves, remembering every
friendship, and understanding at last that beneath all of it, older than any of it, is an
imperative that was never an opinion. Not something the Unremembered believes. Something it is
built on.
