The winter of the drowning (CANON-031) was also the winter of the **Black Surge** — a tidal
catastrophe that took thousands of lives along the coast. The harbour survived it, and never
asked its own survival a second question. The surviving families signed the Charter before the
spring.

Modern citizens understand the Charter as the town's constitution: the Council, harbour
ownership, warning rights, seawall duty. But it carries clauses nobody has ever quite explained.
Certain bells cannot be removed. Certain submerged properties cannot legally be sold. Some
tunnels must remain accessible. No building may rise past seven apparently arbitrary sightlines.
Every harbourmaster must make one inspection a year alone. Certain debts cannot legally be
forgiven. Several paragraphs use words whose meanings are lost.

The Charter is not only a constitution. It is a maintenance manual, written by people who knew
something about what is under the water — not everything, but enough — and knew better than to
write it plainly.

One office inherits more than the rest. The **Keeper of Works** supervises harbour
infrastructure, and every incoming Keeper receives a sealed document from their predecessor:
seventeen instructions, no explanations. Among them:

> NEVER SILENCE ALL BELLS AT ONCE.
> THE EASTERN FOUNDATION MUST REMAIN WET.
> IF CHILDREN REPORT THE BLUE DOOR, CLOSE THE NORTH LOCK.
> DO NOT ALLOW AN ACTUARY INTO THE BELL HOUSE AFTER MIDNIGHT.
> RECORD DREAMLESSNESS.
> IF THE CHORISTER APPEARS INDOORS, EVACUATE THE BUILDING.
> IF THE WATER SPEAKS YOUR NAME, DO NOT ANSWER TWICE.

Centuries ago there were explanations. The explanations were lost; the procedures survived.
Bureaucracy has preserved this better than religion could have — a procedure asks for
compliance, not belief, and compliance keeps.

Beneath Charter Hall, a sealed staircase descends below sea level. The architectural plans
insist it does not exist.
