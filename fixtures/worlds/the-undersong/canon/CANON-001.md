Beneath the harbour lies a drowned god, and it is still singing. The song is slow — one verse a
season — and the harbour's luck rises and falls with the verse. Most people have never heard it.
Everyone has felt it.

**Amended at revision 52**, because "luck" was doing too much work and meant nothing:

The tide in this harbour does not obey the tables. It obeys the verse. One verse a season means a
baseline that drifts on a schedule no almanac can hold, which is why the Vigil's ledger has a
column for what the tables predicted and a column for what the water actually did, and why those
two columns have never once agreed for a whole year.

Almost nobody can hear the song. Everybody lives inside its consequence: a harbour where the
water is persistently, quietly wrong.

Hearing it does two things and only two. You can tell what the water is going to do. And you can
sing back into it and move it — at a cost drawn from everyone within earshot (CANON-046).

What the town calls its good harbour is not geography and not luck. It is two hundred years of
continuous intervention that nobody alive remembers is intervention.

**Further amended at revision 56.** No living theory of what the Undersong *is* has ever been
disproved, which is a different thing from any of them being true. Three read the same two
hundred years of evidence and agree on none of it:

**The Long Grief** holds that the god is dying, still, and has been for two centuries — that a
verse is a stanza of a death that is simply very large and very slow, and that the misfortune it
draws (CANON-046) is grief's ordinary cruelty: it takes from whoever is nearest.

**The Long Labour** holds the opposite: that the drowning (CANON-031) was never a death, and that
something has been coming to term down there ever since, on a schedule the town mistook for
mourning. On this reading, every hearing spent in the harbour has been spent easing a birth
nobody consented to attend.

**The Long Reading** holds that neither death nor birth is happening at all — that the god is
reciting something, and has been the whole time, and that the town's own compulsive
record-keeping — the Vigil's six columns, unbroken since 1747 — is not a human habit imposed on
an indifferent noise. It is imitation. The town keeps a ledger because, at the bottom of the
harbour, so does something else.

No school explains CANON-050. No school explains why there is nothing down there to have died,
been born, or spoken from (CANON-031). And no school has an answer for what happens when a
recitation, or a labour, or a grief, finally reaches its end — a question that, for the first
time in living memory, is no longer rhetorical (CANON-051).

**Further amended at revision 59. This paragraph is the concealed layer — no character in either
production currently has access to all of it (CANON-052).**

None of the three schools was wrong. They were reading the same wound from three different
distances. The Long Grief felt the god's real and ongoing pain at what the cutting cost it, and
called the pain a dying. The Long Labour felt something genuinely coming to term — not a birth of
the god, but the slow individuation of what the god cut away, growing itself a self across two
centuries — and called it the wrong parent. The Long Reading felt, correctly, that a record was
being kept — not a story, not a list, but a suture, verse by verse, each one a stitch — and
mistook the keeping for a recitation.

The town believes the god is the danger it pays to keep quiet. It has always been the opposite:
the god is the reason the danger has stayed as small, as slow, and as survivable as it has. What
the tithe-box calls "the keeping of the water" is a war the god has been quietly winning since
before the charter, at a cost the town mistook, for two hundred years, for the price of an
enemy's mercy rather than the cost of its own protector's wound.

**Further amended at revision 69.** Revision 59, above, spoke of "the god" cutting itself and the
wound being its own. CANON-052's own revision 68 corrects this: the god left, at the Withdrawal
(CANON-053); what remained is an echo the god made to hold a second echo bound (CANON-054). The
three schools still divide the same way and are still each partly right, reread against the
correction. **The Long Grief**, for the loneliness of standing guard on a promise long after the
one who asked for it is gone. **The Long Labour**, for the Unremembered's slow, unwilled
becoming, mistaken for the guardian's own birth. **The Long Reading** — nearest of the three all
along — for a recitation that really is one: not a story and not a list, but standing orders,
still being carried out by the only one left who remembers what they were for.
