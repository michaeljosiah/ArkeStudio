When a new verse arrives, it is written down. This has never once failed to happen, and nobody
has ever been ordered to do it.

A caller on watch when the verse comes will lose an unmeasured stretch of time — long enough to
walk to the desk, uncap the ink, and fill most of a page — and return to herself with the pen
still in her hand and no memory of using it. What is on the page is always legible, always
correctly spelled, and always, without exception, in a hand that does not belong to whoever is
holding the pen.

It is the *same* hand. Not similar. The 1820 entry and the 1901 entry were made by two people who
never met, eighty-one years apart, and a clerk who set them side by side under a lamp could not
tell, from the stroke alone, that they were not written in the same sitting by the same wrist.
This has been checked more than once, by people trained to notice forgery, who did not find one.

Nobody has proposed an explanation that survives being asked a second question. The caller is not
possessed — she remembers everything before and after, only not the writing. The hand is not a
disguise — there is nothing under it to disguise. It is simply not hers, for as long as the page
takes to fill, and then it is hers again, and the ache in her wrist afterward is the kind you get
from something else entirely: from singing, or from shouting, for far longer than she did.

**Amended at revision 62 (CANON-052).** It is not the god's hand, and it is not the caller's. It
is the stitch completing itself, and a stitch needs a hand near the wound to close through, the
way a needle needs whatever thread was already threaded. The same hand across a hundred years is
the same wound, sewn the same way, because a wound this old does not improvise its own closing.
It only knows the one stitch it has always used.
