An echo is not a god. It contains no divine soul, no theology of its own, and often nothing a
person would recognise as a mind. It is closer to an instruction given substance — a fragment of
intent built to keep working after the hand that wrote it has gone.

The distinction matters more than it sounds. A god might change its mind. An echo cannot. A god
might learn. An echo repeats. A god understands context. An echo understands purpose. This makes
echoes weaker than their makers and, in exactly the wrong circumstances, more dangerous: an echo
made to protect will go on protecting after its methods have become monstrous, and an echo made
to destroy will go on attempting it after forgetting why.

Three things are true of every echo the record knows of.

**Purpose.** Each was made around a single imperative — keep, preserve, punish, witness, guide,
restrain, erase. It may grow elaborate behaviour around the imperative. It cannot put the
imperative down.

**Inheritance.** Each keeps some cast of the god that made it. Not memories — preferences,
instincts, ways of perceiving. A sea god's echo measures distance in tides. A god of memory's
echo finds yesterday and tomorrow in adjacent rooms.

**Diminishment.** Echoes do not decay in body, because they have none. They decay in concept.
Centuries wear away language, then identity, then context, until only the command is left. The
old word for this is **the Narrowing**, and it is why the oldest echoes are the most dangerous:
they are on their way to being pure instruction. What the harbour has felt this past year
(CANON-051) is the Narrowing, from inside.
