The Keeper Below cannot speak plainly to a waking mind — waking minds insist on ordinary
language, and it has none. The sleeping mind is less particular.

Throughout the harbour, people occasionally share a dream: flooded streets; a great door
beneath the water; someone standing on the other side of it; a song whose melody cannot be
carried back across waking. Children have it most often. So do the dying. Most people mention
it to nobody, because it does not feel like a dream worth mentioning — it feels like a dream
they have been rude to interrupt.

The **Tide-Called** are the rare few who receive something even while awake. Maren Kest is one.
It is not a power; it is an abnormal sensitivity, and what arrives is not sentences but
impressions — OPEN. DOWN. NOT HIM. SEVEN BECOME SIX. DO NOT LET THE QUIET END. Any of these
could mean a dozen things, and Maren has been wrong about them before.

> Room note: she stays wrong sometimes. The moment she becomes a reliable translator, the
> Keeper becomes a plot device and the dread dies. Her misreadings are load-bearing.

And there is the other side of the coin. The Unremembered almost never dreams (CANON-054). The
Vigil ledger's margins have recorded exactly this, for generations, in a phrase every
watchkeeper copies without asking: *no dreams reported*. The oldest volumes carry the longer
form — **THE ONE WHO DOES NOT DREAM** — and the Keeper of Works' sealed instructions still say
RECORD DREAMLESSNESS (CANON-056). Somebody once understood what was being tracked. Nobody now
does; the column is kept the way the bells are rung, because it has always been kept.

Perrin Tallow does not dream, and believes this is simply how their mind works.
