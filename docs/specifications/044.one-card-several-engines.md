# 1 · Requirements

## 1.1 Summary

Arke installs Ollama, starts it, and dispatches to it. Arke installs ComfyUI, starts it, and
dispatches recipes to it that need most of the graphics card. Nothing coordinates the two, and
three separate paths reach the card without knowing about each other:

- **The queue runs them as independent lanes.** `providerConcurrency: { comfyui: 1, kokoro: 1 }`
  serialises ComfyUI against itself. `ollama` is absent from that map, takes the base concurrency,
  and drains in its own lane. Two jobs the same queue dispatched can be in flight on one card.
- **The authoring harness never enters the queue.** An OpenCode session reaches its configured
  provider directly, so a rule enforced in the dispatcher would not cover the path that holds the
  card longest.
- **Nothing sets a residency hint.** `OllamaClient.submit` posts to `/api/generate` and says
  nothing about `keep_alive`, so the engine's own default applies and weights stay resident for
  minutes after a turn ends.

When this collides, SPEC-021 §2.7's refusal fires and says *close other programs using the
graphics card*. One of those programs is Arke's, and the user cannot close it without closing Arke.

The measurement is not the problem and this specification does not touch it. SPEC-021 §2.7's
distinction between advisory readiness and authoritative dispatch is correct, and reading the
engine's reclaimable reservation rather than raw free VRAM is why a machine holding its own model
cache is not wrongly refused. What is missing is not a number. It is an owner.

A second, smaller absence travels with the first. A local model reaches `ready` from the setup
ledger and nothing ever asks whether it is on the card or on the processor. Ollama will load into
system memory and serve happily, perhaps thirty times slower, with every surface still reading
`ready`. Once an arbiter unloads Ollama before each ComfyUI job, every writing turn becomes a cold
load onto a card that may now be fragmented — which is exactly when a silent fall to CPU happens.
The two belong in one specification because the fix for the first creates the conditions for the
second.

## 1.2 Scope

**In scope.** Ownership of one local accelerator while work runs; the wait a contended claim
produces and how it is stated; unloading the other engine before a claim is granted; measuring
whether a local model is resident on the accelerator and saying so.

**Out of scope.** Whether a recipe can run on this machine at all — that is SPEC-021 §2.7's fit
gate and SPEC-033 §1.5's verdicts, both unchanged. Provider concurrency and the adaptive rate
limiter (SPEC-009 §2.8), which answer a different question about a remote service. Scheduling
policy beyond first-come: no priorities, no pre-emption, no fair-share.

**Deliberately deferred.** Voxa. Kokoro and whisper.cpp are native binaries with a small and
unmeasured accelerator footprint, and admitting them as claimants without that measurement would
serialise dictation behind video generation for no established reason (§1.6, Q-1).

## 1.3 One owner (R-1..R-7)

- **R-1** Local accelerator work SHALL pass through a single **arbiter** that grants exclusive
  ownership of the accelerator to one **claimant** at a time.

- **R-2** The claimants SHALL be named, and SHALL be at least the authoring/text engine and the
  generation engine. A claimant SHALL be identified by the engine it holds, not by the provider
  that dispatched to it (SPEC-033 R-72).

- **R-3** Every path that reaches a local accelerator SHALL claim through the arbiter, including
  paths that do not enter the job queue.
  - The authoring harness is the case this requirement exists for. It holds the accelerator
    longest and is invisible to the dispatcher.

- **R-4** Before ownership is granted to a claimant, the **other** engine SHALL be asked to release
  what it holds, and the release SHALL be attempted unconditionally rather than conditioned on a
  belief that the engine holds something.

- **R-5** The release of the other engine SHALL happen **while the new owner's claim is held**, so
  that a third claim cannot start against a half-released engine.

- **R-6** A claimant SHALL NOT be able to hold the accelerator after its work has reached a
  terminal state, whether that state is success, failure or cancellation.

- **R-7** An engine whose locality is remote (SPEC-033 R-9) SHALL NOT be a claimant, and work
  dispatched to it SHALL NOT wait on this machine's accelerator.
  - A user-directed ComfyUI URL runs on another computer. Making its jobs queue behind a local
    writing turn would be arbitrary, and would make a remote engine slower than no engine.

## 1.4 Contention is a wait, not a refusal (R-8..R-13)

- **R-8** A contended claim SHALL **wait**. It SHALL NOT be refused, and SHALL NOT be answered with
  SPEC-021 §2.7's insufficient-memory sentence.
  - That sentence is for a card that will never be big enough. Reusing it for contention teaches
    the user that their machine is too small when it is merely busy, and invites them to close a
    program that would not have helped.

- **R-9** A waiting claim SHALL state **who holds the accelerator**, in the same capability words
  the engine's own row uses (SPEC-033 R-68).

- **R-10** The wait SHALL be visible where the work is, and SHALL be distinguishable from the work
  itself having started.

- **R-11** A waiting claim SHALL be cancellable by the person who is waiting, and cancelling SHALL
  release the claim without disturbing the current owner.

- **R-12** A claim that cannot be granted within a bounded wait SHALL end as a stated failure naming
  the holder, and SHALL NOT wait indefinitely.

- **R-13** Ownership SHALL be granted in claim order. No claimant SHALL be starved by later claims,
  and no claimant SHALL pre-empt a running one.
  - **WHEN** a writing turn is requested while a video generation is running **THEN** the turn
    waits for that generation and is granted before any generation queued after it.

## 1.5 Residency truth (R-14..R-19)

- **R-14** Whether a loaded local model occupies the accelerator SHALL be **measured from the
  engine**, and SHALL NOT be inferred from a readiness flag this application maintains.
  - A flag goes stale the moment R-4's unconditional release runs, which is the whole point of R-4.

- **R-15** A measurement that reports a loaded model occupying no accelerator memory SHALL be read
  as running on the processor.

- **R-16** A model running on the processor SHALL be **stated and not refused**. Work SHALL proceed.
  - A slow local model beats no local model, and whether to close something and retry is the user's
    call, not this application's.

- **R-17** The statement SHALL say that the model is running on the processor, that it will be slow,
  and the likely reason.

- **R-18** Processor fallback SHALL NOT be an activation state and SHALL NOT set a row to
  `needs-attention` (SPEC-033 R-25). Nothing is broken; a fact about a running model is neither
  activation nor fit.

- **R-19** A measurement taken immediately after a load SHALL tolerate a late report, and SHALL be
  retried once before processor fallback is concluded.
  - Engines report residency after the load returns, not with it. A single reading taken too early
    reports zero for a model that is on the card.

## 1.6 Open questions

- **Q-1 — Does Voxa contend?** Kokoro and whisper.cpp are native binaries; their accelerator
  footprint has not been measured here. Until it is, admitting them as claimants would serialise
  dictation behind video for no established reason, and excluding them risks a third program on the
  card the arbiter does not know about. Resolve by measuring, not by choosing.

- **Q-2 — Does an idle owner hold?** The authoring harness holds a conversation open across many
  turns with long human pauses. Holding ownership across those pauses starves generation; releasing
  between turns makes every turn a cold load. A residency-with-released-ownership middle state is
  the obvious answer and needs measuring against real cold-load costs before it is specified.

- **Q-3 — More than one accelerator.** Everything above says *the* card. A two-card machine could
  run both engines at once, and nothing here expresses that. Deliberately not solved; the
  single-card case is the one that fails today.

## 2.2 Why the arbiter is not the dispatcher

The obvious home is `providerConcurrency`. Add `ollama: 1`, make the two share a lane, done.

It does not work, for a reason that is structural rather than a matter of taste: **the path that
holds the accelerator longest never enters the queue.** An authoring session talks to its provider
directly, and a lane map governs only what the dispatcher dispatches. A rule enforced there would
serialise the two paths that were already the least of the problem, and leave the writing harness —
which holds a model resident across a whole conversation — outside it.

The arbiter therefore sits above both, and both call it. That also keeps SPEC-009's lane machinery
answering the question it was built for: how hard to push a remote service that may rate-limit us.
Sharing the accelerator is not that question, and folding it into the same map would give one number
two meanings.

## 2.3 Why the release is unconditional, and before rather than after

`dispatcher.ts`'s `releaseLane` already asks the generation engine to put its models down when a
lane drains. The instinct is right and the position is wrong in two ways.

It is **fired and forgotten**, so it gates nothing: the next claim does not wait for it, and a
failure to ask changes nothing anyone can observe. And it is **after**, so it depends on the
previous run having drained cleanly — which is exactly what does not happen when a run was
cancelled, crashed, or is still holding a cache deliberately between two queued jobs.

R-4 moves the release to the front of the transaction, where the claimant that needs the memory is
the one that waits for it. R-4's *unconditional* is the same lesson from the other side: any belief
this application holds about what an engine currently has loaded is a cache, and R-4's own release
is the thing that invalidates it. Asking an engine to release nothing costs a call that returns
quickly. Skipping the ask because of a stale belief costs the run.

R-5 closes the window between the two: the release runs while the new owner's claim is already held,
so a third claim cannot slip into a half-released engine.

## 2.4 Why contention queues

The alternative is to refuse a contended claim and let the caller retry, which is cheaper to build
and produces a worse product for a specific reason: **the refusal we already have means something
else.** SPEC-021 §2.7's sentence is about a card that is too small — a permanent fact about a
machine, whose remedies are a smaller model or the cloud. Contention is transient and its remedy is
five seconds of patience.

A user who sees the permanent sentence for a transient cause learns that their machine cannot do
this. That is the wrong lesson, it is not recoverable by trying again, and it points them at closing
a program that was never the problem.

R-9's *who holds it* is what makes the wait tolerable rather than a hang. *Waiting for the graphics
card: local video* is a sentence a person can act on — by waiting, or by cancelling the thing they
care about less. A spinner is not.

## 2.5 Why residency is measured and not remembered

The tempting shape is a boolean: set it true after a successful load, false after an unload, and
read it whenever a claim needs to know. It desynchronises immediately, and R-4 is why. An
unconditional release runs whether or not the flag said anything was loaded; an engine may evict
under memory pressure with nothing told to us; and a keep-alive expiring is a timer we do not hold.

So R-14 reads the engine, and R-19 reads it twice when the answer is zero and the load has just
returned. The double read is not defensive coding — it is a known reporting lag, and a single early
reading reports zero for a model that is on the card.

R-15's inference is worth stating plainly because the failure it catches is silent by construction:
a model loaded into system memory answers every request correctly. There is no error to surface, no
timeout, no degraded output. There is only slowness, which a user attributes to the application.

R-16 and R-18 keep that a statement rather than a refusal. Fit already has vocabulary for *this
machine cannot run this* and it is not what happened here — the machine ran it, on the wrong
processor. Making it an activation state would put a row into `needs-attention` for a model that is
working, and would collide with SPEC-033 R-25's closed list.

## 2.6 What the harness path needs

R-3 is the requirement with the most reach, because the harness's relationship to the accelerator is
unlike the queue's. A dispatch is bounded work with a terminal state. A conversation is many turns
with human-length pauses between them, and it is the pauses that make the policy hard — which is
Q-2, and is not decided here.

What this specification does fix regardless of Q-2's answer: a harness turn that begins while a
generation is running waits for it (R-13) rather than loading a second model onto a full card, and a
generation that begins while a model is resident releases it first (R-4) rather than measuring
around it. Both hold whether ownership is dropped between turns or held across them.

## 2.7 Cost

One lock, two engine calls that already exist on one side, and a wait state on a surface that
already renders working states. The measurement machinery is untouched.

The cost that is not obvious: **every claim now pays a release.** A writing turn following a
generation reloads its model; a generation following a writing turn reloads its weights. On a
machine with room for both, that is a cost paid for nothing — which is the real argument for Q-2's
middle state, and the reason a future amendment may make the release conditional on a *measured*
shortfall rather than unconditional. It is not conditional now because the measurement that would
gate it is the one R-14 says we cannot yet take reliably at claim time.

# 3 · Decisions

**D1 — The arbiter is not `providerConcurrency` (R-1, R-3).** Considered: adding `ollama: 1` to the
lane map and sharing one lane. Rejected — the authoring harness never enters the queue, so the lane
map cannot reach the path that holds the accelerator longest, and folding accelerator ownership into
the rate limiter would give one number two unrelated meanings. §2.2.

**D2 — Contention waits (R-8).** Considered: refusing a contended claim and letting the caller
retry, which is cheaper. Rejected — the refusal we already have means *this card is too small*, a
permanent fact with permanent remedies, and spending it on a five-second collision teaches the user
the wrong thing about their machine. §2.4.

**D3 — The release is unconditional (R-4).** Costs a call that often does nothing. Buys the removal
of every stale-belief failure mode, including the one R-4's own release creates. §2.3.

**D4 — The release runs inside the claim (R-5).** Costs holding the lock across a network call.
Buys the closure of the window in which a third claim starts against a half-released engine. §2.3.

**D5 — Processor fallback is a statement, not a state (R-16, R-18).** Considered: a sixth activation
state, or `needs-attention`. Rejected — SPEC-033 R-25 closed that list deliberately, the model is
working, and a row that demands attention for working software is a false alarm. §2.5.

**D6 — Remote engines are not claimants (R-7).** Follows SPEC-033 R-9's locality boundary rather
than inventing a second one. A remote ComfyUI is on another computer; queueing its work behind this
machine's writing turn would make a remote engine slower than none.

**D7 — Voxa is deferred, not excluded (§1.2, Q-1).** Considered: admitting Kokoro and whisper.cpp as
claimants for completeness. Rejected for now — their accelerator footprint is unmeasured, and
serialising dictation behind video generation on an assumption is a real regression traded for a
hypothetical one. The question is closed by measuring.

**D8 — No priorities, no pre-emption (R-13).** Considered: letting a short writing turn jump a long
video generation, since the user is present for one and not the other. Rejected — pre-empting a
generation wastes accelerator work that has already been paid for in time, and first-come is the
only policy whose behaviour a user can predict.
