The production story workspace

From a world you have
to a scene you can shoot.

Arke already holds a world — cast, locations, canon, a look — and it already knows how to turn an accepted shot into generated footage. This feature is the part in between: creating a production, deciding what it is about, breaking it into episodes and beats, and arriving at scenes that the existing pipeline can shoot. It adds one workspace and one rule about layout, and it changes nothing about how the world is authored.

Status

Designed and drawn, not yet built. Turns 43–48 of the design master specify it and the Prototype makes it clickable. In the shipping application the shells exist — a production, a day one, a Story screen, scenes, shots, dispatch — but the authoring bridge described here is not implemented. The companion brief, production-story-workspace.html, carries a claim ledger recording exactly which parts are built, which are partial, and which are proposed. Read that before estimating anything.

What it is

One creative line, four scopes.

A production is a lens on a world, never a copy of it. Nothing here duplicates a character or a canon entry; a production cites them by id and reads through to whatever they are now. What the workspace adds is the layer of decisions that belong to this film or season and to nothing else.

Season

What changes this time

The question the season answers, the state it ends in, the arcs that run through it, and how it looks. A short film has the same layer with fewer parts.

Episode

One audience promise

Opens, turns, closes. A hook and a cliffhanger. Underneath, the ordered beats that say what changes — and which scenes realise them.

Scene

What happens and is said

Drafted in conversation, accepted into shots. Already implemented, and the point everything above is trying to reach.

The layout rule, because it is the thing most likely to be got wrong. A view that authors one object keeps the split — conversation on the left, a 470px structured draft on the right, its action at the foot. A view whose value comes from reading rows against each other takes the surface — the full 1124px content column, with the conversation reduced to a row at its foot. That is why the Season and Direction views keep the rail while Episodes and Arcs take the page, and why an episode has two views of different shapes. The test is not importance or volume. It is whether you need to compare.
Step one

Creating a production opens it.

Format is step one and decides which surfaces exist: story, video or stills. Profile is step two and only sets defaults — a micro-drama is a Video production with the same scenes, shots, takes and cut as a short film. No second model, no parallel pipeline.

Step two scales to what the profile actually decides. A micro-drama sets aspect, episode count, per-episode length, a hook window, a cliffhanger check, an export preset and which view Story opens on. A short film sets two things and says plainly what it does not set. Where a format has one profile the step does not render at all, because a question with one answer is a toll.

The action names where it lands — Create and open day one, or for a series Create Series and open day one. A button that says Create production and then returns you to a list has told you what it made and not where it went, and the list is the one place the thing you just named is hardest to find.

Day one is never blank

The production opens with the world already in it — cast, locations, canon count, tone, art direction — and with seeds from two sources, always in this order: open canon threads first, then work another production planned and never shot. Threads come first because they are the world asking a question and a production answering one is the whole thesis; uncovered work is opportunistic and reads that way.

When both sources are empty — a young world, one production — each renders as a dashed card saying which source is empty and what would fill it. A page that goes quiet at the exact moment someone starts is the failure day one exists to prevent.
Step two

Story is a conversation beside a draft.

The Story workspace is the third instance of a pattern the product already uses twice, in world Genesis and in World Chat: the same split, the same composer carrying attach and voice, the same rail with its action at the foot. A production screen already owns its 236px rail, so the conversation column is 654px rather than half the page.

Talking writes nothing. Every reply carries a receipt ending nothing written, and the rail shows the accepted draft with proposed changes marked on the fields they would change, each carrying the accepted text beneath it. A draft that showed only the new version would make you hold the old one in your head.

One action turns the draft into one proposal for one file. Accept happens elsewhere, on the proposals screen, which is where every other accepted change in the product is confirmed.

Step three

Four views, and the layout follows what each is for.

The Story workspace has one tab strip: Season, Episodes, Arcs, Direction. A production with no episodes shows three, not four greyed out.

ViewAnswersShape
Season What is this season about, what state does it end in, and what engine does it inherit from the Series? Split · one object
Episodes Do these episodes work as a sequence? Does five earn its place between four and six? Surface · comparison
Arcs What changes across the season, where does it turn, and does it land? Surface · comparison

The episode board

Episodes take the content column as a grid of four tiles at 257px. Eight fit without scrolling and the eighth is Add an episode, last in the grid, so making one happens where the others already are. Each card carries its hook and cliffhanger as two lines, and states a missing ending in words — NO ENDING YET — so colour is never carrying the meaning alone. An unwritten episode is a dashed tile holding a sentence, never an empty slot: the shape of the season stays honest whether or not it has been written.

Inheritance is shown, not hidden

The Season view carries the Series engine as a read-only card naming which seasons it governs, and says that editing it opens the Series. A field that silently edits two seasons is exactly the drift the world model exists to prevent. Direction works the same way: it shows the world look first, then the lines this season narrows against it, and states that no other production moves.

Step four

Beats cite scenes. They never contain them.

An episode opens from a card on the board. Its rail carries the promise first — opens, turn, closes, then hook and cliffhanger — because an episode is one audience promise and its beats only make sense underneath it. Beats come second, ordered, each naming what changes rather than what is seen.

The second view maps beats against scenes, and it is where the structure earns its keep. One beat may be carried by two scenes. One scene may be cited by two episodes — a release that cuts through a continuous production scene, ending episode three inside it and opening episode four where it left off. A beat with no scene says no scene yet and offers to draft one rather than hiding.

Scenes claimed by no beat are surfaced at the foot with a count. A scene that nothing points at is the one thing this view exists to reveal.

This is why the hierarchy is not a folder tree. Arcs, setups and payoffs cross episode boundaries; beats may be realised by several scenes. Arke stores stable links and derives the views, so the same scene appearing under two episodes is correct rather than duplicated.

The boundary

What can change anything.

The rules here are inherited, not invented. This feature adds surfaces; it does not widen what an agent may touch.

Conversation

Proposes, never writes

A turn may read the world and the production and suggest changes. It cannot write an accepted file, accept a proposal, or dispatch work. Receipts state what was read and that nothing was written.

The accept gate

One path to truth

Every authored change becomes a staged proposal holding whole files, verified against the base it was drafted from, and accepted explicitly. Story, episode and direction changes use the same path as canon and sheets.

Dispatch

The only spend

Accepting a scene creates its shots and dispatches nothing. Money moves only at an explicit dispatch, with the estimate and every warning computed first.

World facts

Cross over separately

A production conversation that surfaces something true of the whole world does not write it. It becomes a world change, reviewed on its own terms.

Impact

Files and folders.

The world folder is the only durable truth, readable by hand, with no git. What follows is what a production directory holds today, verified against the code and against the sample world in fixtures/worlds/the-undersong — then what this feature adds.

A day in the life

Thursday, with The Undersong.

One morning making a seven-episode micro-drama in a world that already exists, including the part where the season is wrong and has to be fixed. Every step says what reached disk, because that is the promise this feature has to keep. Written in the present tense as though it were built; it is not yet.

09:38 · creating

A world she has, an idea she half has.

The Undersong has six characters, four locations and forty-one canon entries. She wants something vertical and nightly out of it. New production, Video, then step two: Micro-drama series. Seven episodes, 45–75 seconds, hook in three, cliffhanger required. She types the idea the way she would say it aloud — a bell-ringer on the late watch starts getting answers back.

The button says Create Series and open day one, so she knows where she is going.

On disk: productions/bell-watch-s1/production.json created, carrying the profile and its defaults. series/bell-watch.json created with the two of them linked. One line appended to changes.jsonl. Nothing generated, nothing copied out of the world.
09:39 · arriving

Day one, and the world walked in with her.

Six faces, The Vigil, The Drowned Quarter, tone, art direction v3. Two groups of seeds: an open canon thread about what the water leaves behind, and a scene Saltlight accepted and never shot. She reads them and takes neither.

On disk: nothing. Day one is a read.
09:52 · the season

The engine is not the question.

In Story she says the season should be about who is answering, not whether her mother is alive. The reply separates the two: the nightly answer is the Series engine and season two needs it intact; what she has changed is the season question and its ending. The rail marks both fields, showing the accepted text beneath each.

She presses Turn this into a proposal, reads the diff, accepts.

On disk: while talking, nothing. On staging, .proposals/pr_.../ holds the whole proposed season.json. On accept, season.json is written, the previous version lands in .history/, and changes.jsonl gains a line. The Series engine is untouched — it would have needed its own accept.
10:14 · the board

Seven cards, and two of them are lying.

Episodes takes the page. Seven tiles plus Add an episode. Five have hooks, four have cliffhangers, and episode three says NO ENDING YET in words rather than in red. Episode six is a dashed tile with one sentence, which is honest: it has a title and nothing else.

On disk: nothing. The board is a view over the episode files.
10:26 · the episode

It opens well and stops.

She clicks episode three. The rail shows its promise: they go back to the night the ledger skips, and then nothing. She says the street is there but the house numbers are wrong. The reply makes that the turn, and gives the episode a closing state it did not have — the Quarter has been edited, and someone did it — which pays off the ledger from episode two.

Two new beats appear at the bottom of the list, both marked no scene yet.

On disk: nothing until she stages it. On accept, episodes/03-the-missing-night.json is written with its promise and four ordered beats. The two new beats cite no scene, and the file says so.
10:41 · the scene

A beat with no scene offers to become one.

On the beats-and-scenes view, beat three is a dashed row reading Draft the scene for this beat. She presses it and the beat becomes the brief. The draft comes back with a location it inherits, a cast resolved from @ mentions, and four shots — and it names the canon it checked.

She accepts. The shots exist. Nothing has been generated.

On disk: scenes/11-the-answer-from-inside.json written, with shots[] carrying camera, audio and duration. Episode three's beat three now cites sc_11. Still no money spent.
10:58 · the arc that never lands

The grid tells her something the board could not.

Arcs, across the season. Three lanes. Maren's turns in episode four and pays off in seven. The Quarter's does the same. Odile's lane has a setup, a turn in five — and the finale column reads NO PAYOFF. She has been believed and disbelieved and the season never says which she is.

She types into the row at the foot: Odile's lane needs to land somewhere.

On disk: nothing yet. Lanes and their cells live in season.json; the missing payoff is computed, not stored.
11:20 · the first spend

One shot, priced before it runs.

Scene eleven, shot two. The dispatch dialog names the model, the size and the length it will actually ask for, and lists what it knows: one sheet is a sketch, one reference will be dropped for exceeding the model's cap. None of it blocks. She presses dispatch.

On disk: a job appended to %USERPROFILE%\ArkeStudio\queue\jobs.jsonl before any network call, then the artifact into productions/bell-watch-s1/incoming/sh_02/, then a take under takes/tk_.../. Accepting it writes selections.json and appends to reviews.jsonl in one commit. The scene file does not move — it is gated, and a selection is operational.
Three hours, one accepted season, one accepted episode, one accepted scene, one clip. The world it all came from was not edited once — and the two moments that could have edited it, the canon thread on day one and the checked canon during the scene draft, both stayed reads.
Honest limits

What this does not do, and what is not decided.

Deliberately out of scope

No branching, no graph, no choice or state. A season is a sequence, and the graph language the game prototype proves is not copied into linear video on day one. No world editing from a production conversation. No generation from unaccepted state. Nothing here changes canon, sheets or the world look.

Not yet designed

The scene script layer — stable action and dialogue blocks with shots citing the blocks they cover — is specified in the brief and not drawn. Neither is the pass planner, the durable dispatch plan, the Series view for when season two exists, or an episode-aware cut and export. Seven episodes are seven deliverables and the cut is still one timeline.

Known to be unresolved

The Prototype now has two step-two screens for Video: this feature's profile step, and an older subtype screen offering Watch, Choose and Interact. Those are two different axes — form and interactivity — and nothing has reconciled them. A season of twenty is the first thing that will test the episode grid and the arc columns, both of which are drawn at seven. And the conversation layer this feature consumes exists only for the world; extending it to production scope is real work, bounded but not free.
