The production story workspace

From a world you have
to a scene you can shoot.

Arke already holds your world: characters, places, canon, a look. It already knows how to turn a finished shot into video. This is the part in between — deciding what a film or a season is about, breaking it into episodes and scenes, and writing the script the shots will cover.

Status

Designed, not built. The pictures below are the real frames from the design master, turns 43–50, so what you see is exactly what was specified. The shipping app has the shells — a production, a day one, a Story screen, scenes, dispatch — but not this. The companion production-story-workspace-brief.html tracks what is actually built.

What it is

Four things to decide, in order.

A production borrows from a world. It never copies it. Change a character once and every production sees it. What this feature adds is the layer of decisions that belong to this film and nothing else.

Season

What changes this time

The question it answers, how it ends, the arcs running through it, and how it looks. A short film has the same thing with fewer parts.

Episode

One promise to the audience

How it opens, where it turns, how it closes. A hook and a cliffhanger. Then the beats that say what changes.

Scene

What happens and is said

The script, then the shots that cover it. This is where the existing pipeline picks up.

One rule explains every layout here. If a screen is for writing one thing, the conversation sits on the left and a 470px draft panel on the right. If a screen is for comparing things to each other, it takes the full width and the conversation shrinks to a single row at the bottom. That is why Episodes and Arcs get the whole page, and Season, Direction and Script keep the panel. The test is not how important a screen is. It is whether you need to read rows against each other.
Starting

Creating a production opens it.

Three screens before there is anything to write in. Pick what you are making, pick what kind of it, then land on day one with the world already there.

Step one · what are you making?

Format is the first choice and the structural one. Story, video or stills. It decides which screens exist for the rest of the production, and it is the only choice here that does: a story gets chapters, stills gets a contact sheet, video gets scenes, shots and a cut.

Nothing is copied out of the world. Whichever you pick, the production shares the same characters, places and canon, and picks up changes to them as they happen.

Step two · what kind of video?

Format decides structure. Profile only sets defaults. This is where the video format is defined: the frame it is made for, how many episodes, how long each runs, whether a cliffhanger is expected.

A micro-drama is still a Video production, with the same scenes, shots, takes and cut as a short film. It just starts with different numbers. Every default is a chip you can open and change, here or later.

A short film sets two things and states plainly what it does not set. If a format has only one profile the step does not appear at all, because a question with one answer is a toll.

Step three · arriving

The button says where it goes: Create and open day one, or for a series Create Series and open day one. It does not drop you back on a list.

Day one is never blank. If both seed sources are empty — a young world with one production — each says which one is empty and what would fill it. A page that goes quiet the moment someone starts is the thing day one exists to prevent.

The workspace

A conversation next to a draft.

Story works the same way as the two conversation screens Arke already has. You talk on the left. The draft builds on the right. Nothing is written until you turn it into a proposal.

  • Talking writes nothing. Every reply ends with a receipt saying so.
  • Changes are marked on the field they affect, with the current text below.
  • One button turns the draft into one proposal for one file.
  • Accepting happens on the proposals screen, like every other change in Arke.
The season

Four views of the same season.

Season, Episodes, Arcs and Direction. A production with no episodes shows three, not four with one greyed out.

Episodes takes the whole page because the point is comparing them — whether episode five earns its place between four and six. You cannot answer that if you can only see five of seven. Unwritten episodes are dashed tiles with a sentence in them, so the shape of the season is honest before it is written.

A lane is a thing that changes, not a character. Maren appears in two of the rows above and is neither of them. The finale column is where you find out whether an arc actually lands.

What a season inherits

The Season view shows the Series engine as a read-only card, and says that editing it opens the Series. A field that quietly edits two seasons at once is exactly the kind of drift the world model exists to stop. Direction works the same way: it shows the world look first, then the lines this season narrows against it, and says plainly that no other production moves.

The episode

Beats point at scenes. They do not own them.

You open an episode from a card on the board. The panel shows its promise first, then the beats underneath.

A beat says what changes, not what you see. The second view maps beats against scenes, and that is where the structure pays off:

  • One beat can be carried by two scenes.
  • One scene can be used by two episodes — a release that cuts through the middle of a continuous scene.
  • A beat with no scene says so, and offers to write one.
  • Scenes that no beat points at are listed at the bottom. Those are the ones worth knowing about.
The scene

Script first. Shots second.

A scene is written before anything decides how to film it. Then shots are added, and each one says which parts of the script it covers.

Block ids are given once and never renumbered. Insert a block between A03 and A04 and it becomes A08. That looks odd in a list, and it is the whole point: renumbering would silently change what every shot is pointing at.

Dialogue lives in the script block. The shot points at it rather than repeating the line, so there is only ever one copy of what is said.

When the script changes

Editing a covered block does not break anything. The block keeps its id, and any shot pointing at it is marked covers text that changed — out of date, not broken. Cutting a block leaves the shot in place marked covers nothing. It is kept, not deleted, because you framed and timed it and that is not Arke's to throw away.

Splitting a block is not a special case. It is an edit plus an insert, and you have already seen what both do. The one thing Arke cannot work out is which half a shot was for, so it asks.

The board

The fourth tab is the visual read: every shot in scene order, at the shape it will be watched in. A 9:16 production boards portrait, so the frame chosen back at step two is visible here rather than discovered at dispatch.

  • Compiling is free and local. The same scene state always makes the same board, and it files nothing.
  • Exporting files one artifact. That is why they are two buttons: you can recompile twenty times and nothing accumulates.
  • A gap is a panel. A shot with no frame keeps its place, number, duration and coverage, because a half-generated scene still has to read in order.
  • Staleness is per panel. A script edit dims the panels it actually touched and leaves the rest alone.

This board is not what gets sent to a model. It is editorial, it may hold placeholders, and the provider-ready storyboard is compiled separately at dispatch. Two artefacts, one shot plan.

Nothing on these screens blocks you. Every warning names the specific block or shot and lets you carry on.
Before you spend

See the whole bill first.

The plan is one screen showing every shot in the scene, what each one will send, and what each one will cost. Nothing has been sent yet. Nothing has been charged.

You reach it from Generate in the left rail. It replaces the old warning box that only appeared once you had already pressed the button.

  • Shots stay in scene order. Left to right is the order they will be watched in.
  • Each shot shows the script it covers without you clicking anything. If shot 2 covers a line of dialogue, the line is on the card.
  • Each shot shows its own price. The total at the top is the sum, and the button repeats it: Generate 4 shots · $5.46.
  • Script with no shot is shown as a gap. The dashed row at the bottom names the blocks nobody is filming, and offers to add a shot.

Warnings sit in one bar above the shots. They name the thing they are about — a missing shot, a character who is still a sketch — and none of them stop you.

Where the numbers come from: a provider only makes a few fixed lengths. Ask for 3 seconds on a route whose shortest is 4 and you are charged for 4. The card says so on its face, so a 3-second shot that bills as 4 seconds is visible here rather than on the invoice.

One shot, opened

Click a shot and you get the prompt Arke is about to send, split into the six parts it is built from. Every part names where it came from.

  • Style — the art direction, or the world's tone and genre if there is none.
  • Location — the place, plus the first line of its look.
  • Time — the scene's time of day.
  • Action — the shot description, with every @name swapped for that character's appearance.
  • Camera and Audio — whatever the shot itself says.

Because each row is sourced, a wrong prompt has a findable cause. If the style reads wrong, the art direction is wrong, and that is where you go and fix it. You are not editing text that came from nowhere.

Underneath is what travels with the prompt: the reference images. Some are carried, some are dropped, and the dropped ones say why — usually that the provider takes fewer images than the shot has, so the lowest-priority one is cut. Next to that are the settings: model, size, length, price.

This screen is computed fresh each time you open it, from the world as it stands right now. It is a forecast, not a record. What was actually sent is frozen at dispatch and belongs to the take.
The boundary

What can actually change things.

These rules are inherited from the rest of Arke. This feature adds screens; it does not widen what an agent may touch.

  • Conversation proposes, never writes. A reply can read the world and suggest changes. It cannot write a file, accept a proposal or spend money.
  • The accept gate is the only path. Every authored change becomes a staged proposal holding whole files, checked against the version it was drafted from.
  • Accepting a scene creates shots and dispatches nothing. Money moves only at an explicit dispatch, with the estimate shown first.
  • World facts cross over separately. If a production conversation turns up something true of the whole world, it becomes a world change and is reviewed on its own.

A day in the life

Thursday, with The Undersong.

One morning making a seven-episode micro-drama in a world that already exists, including the part where the season is wrong. Each step says what reached disk. Written as though it were built.

09:38 · creating

A world she has, an idea she half has.

New production, Video, then Micro-drama series. Seven episodes, 45 to 75 seconds, vertical. She types the idea the way she would say it out loud: a bell-ringer on the late watch starts getting answers back.

On disk: production.json and series/bell-watch.json created. One line in changes.jsonl. Nothing generated.
09:39 · arriving

Day one, and the world came with it.

Six faces, three locations, the tone, art direction v3. Two seed groups: an open canon thread, and a scene Saltlight accepted and never shot. She reads both and takes neither.

On disk: nothing. Day one only reads.
09:52 · the season

The engine is not the question.

She says the season is about who is answering, not whether her mother is alive. The reply separates them: the nightly answer is the Series engine and season two needs it. What changed is the season question and its ending.

On disk: nothing while talking. On accept, season.json written, old version to .history/. The Series engine untouched — that needs its own accept.
10:14 · the board

Seven cards, and two of them are lying.

Five have hooks, four have cliffhangers. Episode three says NO ENDING YET. Episode six is a dashed tile with one sentence, which is honest.

On disk: nothing. The board is a view over the episode files.
10:26 · the episode

It opens well and stops.

She opens episode three and says the street is there but the house numbers are wrong. That becomes the turn, and gives the episode a closing state it did not have. Two new beats appear, both with no scene.

On disk: on accept, episodes/03-the-missing-night.json with four ordered beats. Two cite no scene, and say so.
10:41 · the scene

A beat with no scene offers to become one.

She presses Draft the scene for this beat. The beat becomes the brief. Seven script blocks come back, with the canon it checked named. She accepts, then adds four shots that cover five of them.

On disk: scenes/11-the-answer-from-inside.json with its blocks and shots. The beat now cites sc_11. No money spent.
10:58 · the arc that never lands

The grid says what the board could not.

Three lanes. Two turn and pay off. Odile's has a setup, a turn in episode five, and NO PAYOFF in the finale column. She has been believed and disbelieved and the season never decides which.

On disk: nothing yet. Lanes live in season.json; the missing payoff is worked out, not stored.
11:20 · the first spend

Four shots, priced before any of them run.

She opens Generate and gets the whole bill: four shots, the script each one covers, $5.46 in total. Two things are flagged and neither blocks — two blocks have no shot, and Odile is still a sketch. Shot 3 says it will bill as 4 seconds because 3 is shorter than the route makes. She opens shot 2, reads the prompt row by row, sees that one of its three references will be dropped and why, and sends all four.

On disk: a job in queue/jobs.jsonl before any network call, then the file into incoming/, then a take. Accepting writes selections.json and reviews.jsonl together. The scene file does not move.
Three hours. One season, one episode, one scene, one clip. The world was never edited — including the two moments that could have edited it, the canon thread on day one and the canon checked while drafting, which both stayed reads.
Limits

What this does not do.

On purpose

No branching and no graph. A season is a sequence. No editing the world from a production conversation. No generating from anything unaccepted.

Not designed yet

The pass planner, and saving a plan. The plan screen works out its numbers fresh every time you open it; nothing writes down what you intended to send. A Series view for when season two exists. An episode-aware cut and export — seven episodes are seven deliverables and the cut is still one timeline.

There is also no after view. You can read a shot's prompt before it runs, but once a take exists there is no screen that shows what was actually sent. Part of that is a gap in the data: a take records the canon version, the sheet versions and the art direction version it was made from, but not which reference images travelled. That cannot be worked out later, because the world has moved on. So the record has to start being kept before the screen can exist.

Unresolved

There are two step-two screens for Video: this feature's profile step, and an older one offering Watch, Choose and Interact. Those are different questions — form and interactivity — and nothing has reconciled them. A season of twenty is the first thing that will test the episode grid and the arc columns, both drawn at seven.
