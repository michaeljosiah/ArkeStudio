Files on disk

Two folders hold everything.

Arke Studio keeps nothing in a private database you cannot open. There is one folder for the application and one folder per world, and between them they hold the lot. This is the reference page: open your own folder, find a name you do not recognise, look it up here.

The first folder is Arke's home: keys, preferences, what has been spent, what has been queued. The second is a world, and that is the one that matters, because it is the thing you own.

Every path here was checked against the running code and the sample world that ships with the source. Where the sample world has no such file, that is not an omission — Arke never creates an empty folder. One appears the first time an operation needs to put something in it.

Arke's home on your machine

One folder, in your user folder, called ArkeStudio. Arke creates it the first time it runs and never asks you where to put it. Nothing in here is your writing. Your writing is one level down, inside a world.

The location can be moved. Arke reads a setting called ARKE_STUDIO_ROOT — an environment variable — and if it holds a path, that path replaces the default entirely. That is how a test copy runs beside a real one, and it is how you would put Arke on a second drive.

ArkeStudio\ in your user folder, unless ARKE_STUDIO_ROOT says otherwise config.json app config, written on first launch settings.json your preferences: which model goes where, the spend alert credentials.dat your provider keys, encrypted for your account alone grants.json permissions you told Arke to remember ledger.jsonl every charge, one line each, across every world queue\ jobs.jsonl every generation job and each state it passed through provider-calls\ calls.jsonl recent calls, redacted, for when something goes wrong logs\ app.jsonl faults, alerts, validation runs coordinator.jsonl what the engine did desktop.jsonl what the window did harness.jsonl what the assistant's adapter did; rolls over at 5 MB run\ children.json helper processes Arke started, so none is left orphaned models\ local runtimes and voices you chose to download update\ pending.json a receipt for a staged update: read once, then removed .spool\ a pasted file, on its way into a world .genesis\ a conversation about a world that does not exist yet .art\ .extract\ scratch space for the look helper and fact extraction .index\ app.db a listing cache over the logs and the worlds present worlds\ the-undersong\ one world, one folder. this is your work another-world\ archive\ worlds you archived: moved whole, never deleted exports\ whole-world copies, one stamped folder each

What each one is, and what losing it costs

PathWhat it isIf you lose it
config.json App configuration, created on first launch. Never holds a key. Nothing. Written again on the next start.
settings.json Your preferences: which model handles which kind of job, the spend alert, theme, local runtime choices. Preferences only. They go back to their defaults.
credentials.dat Your provider keys, encrypted for your account. The file's permissions are reset to you alone on every write. The keys. Paste them in again. They are never written into a world, so no export can carry them out.
grants.json Permissions you told Arke to remember. Revoking marks a grant revoked; it does not erase the record that you gave it. Arke asks you again the next time.
ledger.jsonl One line per finished metered job, across every world, including the ones that failed or you cancelled. Your spending history. The work itself is untouched.
queue/jobs.jsonl Every generation job and every state it passed through. Added to, never rewritten. The trail of what was asked for. Anything in flight loses its record.
provider-calls/calls.jsonl The redacted request and reply of recent calls, so a failure can be explained. Capped: past 2,000 records or 50 MB the oldest go. Restricted to your account. Nothing but the ability to diagnose a recent failure.
logs/ Four running logs, added to a line at a time. Nothing.
run/children.json Which helper processes Arke started, so a crash cannot leave one running forever. Nothing. A stale one is swept at the next start.
models/ Local runtimes and voices you chose to download. A download lands under a temporary name and only takes its real name once it is whole and checked. Disk space and a re-download.
update/pending.json A receipt saying an update is staged and whether it lands on restart or on close. The next start reads it, acts, and removes it. Nothing. The update is offered again.
.spool/ A file pasted from the clipboard, given a real name so the ordinary filing path can take it. Swept at the next start. Nothing.
.index/app.db A search and listing cache built from the logs and the worlds present. Its database may leave two small companion files beside it. Nothing. Deleting it is a supported thing to do; the next start rebuilds it.
worlds/ Your work. One folder per world. Everything
archive/ Worlds you archived. Archiving closes the world and moves the whole folder here — it is a rename, not a delete. Everything in them
exports/ Whole-world copies you asked for, one time-stamped folder each. Copies only. The originals are untouched.
.genesis/ .art/ .extract/ Scratch space: a conversation about a world that does not exist yet, the art-direction helper, and fact extraction from a document. Nothing. A genesis sandbox is removed once the world is begun; the other two are not yet swept automatically.

Everything above the dotted names is your work, in a form you could have typed yourself. The dotted names are Arke's bookkeeping.

The world's own record, the bible, and the look

world.json

The world's identity and its two counters. canonRevision is a clock that ticks once every time a canon change is accepted, so any file can say which state of the world it was made against. nextCanonId is the next number to hand out.

the-undersong/world.json { "worldId": "01J8F3K2QW9VZX4N7M0RTYB6HC", "slug": "the-undersong", "name": "The Undersong", "logline": "A drowned god still sings beneath the harbour.", "tone": "quiet dread", "genre": "coastal fantasy", "canonRevision": 104, "nextCanonId": 72 }

bible.md

Your own prose about the world, and the one file the product invites you to open in a text editor. Save it while Arke is running and it quietly takes the new text. Every save cuts a version and keeps the outgoing text, so an edit nobody wanted is one restore away. A world without a bible is ordinary, not broken.

the-undersong/bible.md --- version: 7 updated: "2026-08-07" --- The harbour was here before the town and will be here after it. Everything the town believes about the water it learned from people who drowned...

world-art.png and art-direction/

The look is one image and one description. The image is the master look every generated picture inherits; the description is the sentence a model is actually given. The record keeps every earlier version beside the current one. Covered in the world.

the-undersong/art-direction/art-direction.json { "version": 3, "description": "Painterly, tidal, restrained. Weathered maritime realism, visible brushwork, slate and sea-glass colour...", "masterLook": "world-art.png", "acceptedAt": "2026-07-18T10:00:00Z", "history": [ { "version": 1, "description": "First world image", ... }, { "version": 2, "description": "Cold-water realism", ... } ] }

Canon, and the three kinds of sheet

canon/ holds what is true about the world: one file per statement, numbered, never renumbered. The header carries the few facts a computer needs — what it links to, which revision introduced it, which revisions amended it — and everything below is ordinary prose.

the-undersong/canon/CANON-044.md --- id: CANON-044 type: thread title: Who taught the Chorister? status: open introducedAt: 41 amendedAt: 71 links: [the-chorister, maren-kest, CANON-001, CANON-051, CANON-052] --- The Chorister knows Maren's true note, and true notes are taught, not overheard. Nobody has settled who taught them, or when, or what was paid for the lesson...

characters/, locations/ and factions/ are the same shape as each other: one Markdown file per thing, a header of facts, prose underneath. The version number in the header is the sheet's own and rises by one every time you accept a change to it. See the world for what each section is for.

the-undersong/characters/maren-kest.md --- id: maren-kest type: character name: Maren Kest role: Tide-caller billing: lead version: 5 status: locked voice: { provider: elevenlabs, voiceId: v_8Kq2, label: Low tide } canonRules: [CANON-002, CANON-058] links: [bray-half-hitch, the-chorister] --- ## Essence Tide-caller. She hears the verse under the harbour and pulls the water where it needs to go. She has paid for it: her left ear is done...

Reference kits, and artifacts

A reference kit is the pile of pictures that keeps a character recognisable across hundreds of generations. Each sheet gets a folder: the tiles, a record of which tile is locked and what made it, and the compiled sheet that travels with a generation request. Covered in the world.

the-undersong/references/maren-kest/kit.json { "sheetId": "maren-kest", "anchor": "head-front.png", "tiles": [ { "angle": "head-front", "status": "locked", "file": "head-front.png" }, { "angle": "body-full", "status": "generated", "file": "body-full.png" }, { "angle": "head-profile", "status": "empty" } ], "compilations": [ { "file": "model-sheet-v4.png", "format": "character-sheet", "sheetVersion": 4, "accepted": true } ], "designatedCompilation": "model-sheet-v4.png" }

artifacts/ holds files you brought in — a sound, a photograph, a treatment as a document. The file keeps the name you recognise, and beside it sits a small card of the same name with .json on the end: where it came from, what it is, what it is linked to. Filing copies your file; the original on your desk is never touched or moved.

the-undersong/artifacts/harbour-bells.wav.json { "id": "ar_01J8G0000000000000000000R1", "kind": "audio", "file": "harbour-bells.wav", "hash": "sha256:6a1e02b9c44d7f31", "origin": { "by": "user" }, "links": ["the-vigil", "CANON-001"], "created": "2026-06-11T10:00:00Z" }

The card names a file, never a path — which is one of the two reasons a world folder can be moved anywhere and still work.

Inside a production

A production is a thing you make from the world: a film, a novel, a series. Making one creates a single file. Nothing is pre-built — no empty scenes folder, no take directories, no copies of your sheets. Productions covers the working shape; takes and the cut covers what happens once pictures start arriving.

productions\saltlight\ production.json title, format, status: the only file made at creation story.json the spine and the acts, as structure story.md the treatment, as prose routing.json which model this production sends its work to spine.json the beat map, once one has been drawn scenes\ 02-the-tables-say-neap.json the scene, and every shot inside it 04-the-verse-rises.json 06-slack-water.json chapters\ prose productions use these instead of scenes 01-neap.md 02-the-same-ink.md 03-nothing-wrong-with-it.md takes\ tk_01J8A0000000000000000000A1\ take.json what was asked, what it cost, what it was made against frame.png the media itself tk_01J8F0000000000000000000B2\ selections.json which take is the chosen one, per shot reviews.jsonl every accept and reject, with the reason cut.json the audio tracks and where they sit boards\ scene-2.png a compiled board for one scene storyboards\ panels drawn for those boards audio\ generated dialogue incoming\ media that has landed but is not yet a take season.json episode order, for an episodic production episodes\ 01-neap.json one file per episode

A scene holds its shots inside it. There are no separate shot files — a shot is an entry in the scene, which is why reordering shots never moves anything on disk.

productions/saltlight/scenes/02-the-tables-say-neap.json { "id": "sc_02", "number": 2, "title": "The tables say neap", "status": "accepted", "version": 3, "inherits": { "location": "the-vigil", "timeOfDay": "dusk" }, "shots": [ { "id": "sh_06", "number": 6, "title": "The waterline disagrees", "description": "From the rail, the marker post at the harbour mouth. The tide is a hand-span above where the tables put it.", "camera": "MS - slow push-in", "audio": { "kind": "sfx", "line": "water against stone, too high" }, "durationSec": 6 } ] }

A take is one attempt at one shot. The media sits in the take's own folder, and beside it a record of what produced it — including which canon revision and which sheet versions it was made against, so an old take can always explain itself.

productions/saltlight/takes/tk_01J8A0000000000000000000A1/take.json { "id": "tk_01J8A0000000000000000000A1", "coversShots": ["sh_12"], "kind": "frame", "provider": "fal", "model": "flux-pro-1.1", "provenance": { "canonRevision": 41, "sheets": { "maren-kest": 4, "the-vigil": 2 } }, "prompt": "Maren Kest at the black stone rail of the Vigil at night...", "references": ["references/maren-kest/model-sheet-v4.png"], "cost": { "estimatedMicroUsd": 40000, "actualMicroUsd": 38000 }, "media": "frame.png" }

Choosing between takes writes two files and never touches the takes themselves. selections.json is the small answer — which take won, per shot. reviews.jsonl is the durable record of every decision, including the ones you reversed.

productions/saltlight/selections.json { "sh_12": { "acceptedTakeId": "tk_01J8F0000000000000000000B2", "startFrameTakeId": "tk_01J8A0000000000000000000A1" } }
productions/saltlight/reviews.jsonl {"ts":"2026-07-30T13:58:02Z","takeId":"tk_...C3","shotId":"sh_12","decision":"reject", "by":"user","citation":{"sheet":"maren-kest","field":"appearance", "note":"coat drifted off-sheet"}} {"ts":"2026-07-30T14:04:11Z","takeId":"tk_...B2","shotId":"sh_12","decision":"accept", "by":"user"}

cut.json holds only the sound: the audio tracks and where they sit, plus any clip you placed by hand. The picture order is not stored, because the scenes and selections.json already answer it — storing it again would give two answers to one question.

productions/saltlight/cut.json { "audio": [ { "kind": "score", "label": "Underscore", "entries": [ ... ] } ], "overlays": [] }

Finished renders do not land inside the production. They are written to exports/ at the top of the world, next to productions/, after being encoded somewhere temporary first — so a cancelled or failed render leaves nothing behind that looks like a finished film.

changes.jsonl: what happened, in order

One line per accepted change, oldest first, for the life of the world. It says what changed, from which version to which, which fields moved, where the change came from, and what the canon revision stood at afterwards. Nothing ever edits an earlier line.

the-undersong/changes.jsonl {"ts":"2026-07-12T11:30:00Z","entity":"canon/CANON-044","fromVersion":null, "toVersion":41,"fieldsChanged":["body"],"source":"chat:sess_3aa", "canonRevisionAfter":41} {"ts":"2026-07-14T09:00:00Z","entity":"characters/maren-kest","fromVersion":3, "toVersion":4,"fieldsChanged":["appearance","voice-written"], "source":"chat:sess_9f2","canonRevisionAfter":41,"proposalId":"pr_...P0"}

Wrapped to fit. On disk each record is one line. The sample world has 104 of them.

The folders that begin with a dot, and the lock

These are Arke's own bookkeeping. You never have to open them, but nothing in them is hidden from you, and two of them are not caches at all.

.history/
A complete copy of every version ever accepted — not a patch, the whole file. This is the version record, and it is not derived: deleting it loses history that nothing can rebuild. A whole-world export includes it deliberately.
.proposals/
Changes waiting for you. One folder per proposal, each holding the complete proposed files rather than a description of edits. Accepting removes the folder; discarding removes it too and writes a line saying so. See the accept gate.
.conversations/
Your chats with Arke about this world: one folder each, holding the conversation's own small record and every event in it, a line at a time. See the assistant.
.sessions/
Bench sessions — working history of the same kind, kept beside conversations.
.commit/
One change part-way through being written. Empty the rest of the time. Opening the world finishes or undoes whatever it finds here before anything reads.
.index/
The search cache, plus a list of what every file looked like when Arke last had the world open. Pure cache: delete it and the next open rebuilds it from the real files.
.cache/
Voice previews, video being encoded, working copies. Rebuilt on demand.
.staging/
Where a generated file lands and is checked before it is allowed into the world.
incoming/
Candidates waiting on your decision — a proposed world image, an image staged for a generation to look at.
world.lock
Present only while the world is open, so a second copy of Arke cannot write to the same world at the same time. It records which process holds it and refreshes while open; a lock left behind by a crash is reclaimed rather than locking you out of your own work. It holds no machine name.

Three kinds of file, and why each is used where it is

KindUsed forWhy that kind
Markdown with a header
.md		 Characters, locations, factions, canon entries, chapters, your bible. The prose is the point, and prose should be readable. The header at the top holds the few facts a computer needs — a name, a version number, what it links to — and the rest of the file is just writing.
Structured data
.json		 The world's own record, productions, scenes, takes, the look, the picture kits, the cards beside artifacts. Structure with no prose in it. Still plain text you can read, still editable by hand if you must, but nothing here is meant to be read as writing.
One line per event
.jsonl		 The change log, the spend ledger, the job log, review decisions, conversation events. Things that only ever get added to. See below.

The third kind deserves a proper explanation, because it is the reason a crash is boring rather than frightening. A file of this kind is only ever appended to. Nothing rewrites an earlier line, nothing renumbers, nothing compacts in place. So the worst an interrupted write can do is leave the last line half-finished — it cannot reach back and damage the history above it.

And a half-finished last line is tolerated rather than fatal. When Arke reads the file it discards a truncated final line and keeps everything before it. When it next writes, it finishes the broken line off with a line break before adding anything new, so the ruined record can never be merged into the next valid one. Losing the last line is the entire cost.

What is safe to delete

Everything derived rebuilds. Nothing you authored lives only in a cache. The rule Arke holds itself to is that deleting any of the green rows below costs you a rebuild and nothing else.

<world>/.index/ Safe The search cache and the last-seen file list. The next open rebuilds both from the real files. There are tests whose whole job is proving this.
ArkeStudio/.index/app.db Safe The cross-world listing cache. Rebuilt at the next start from the logs and the worlds present.
<world>/.cache/ Safe Voice previews and encoding scratch. Made again on demand.
<world>/.staging/ Safe when closed Generated files being checked. Deleting it mid-generation loses that generation, nothing more.
ArkeStudio/logs/ Safe Diagnostics only. You lose the ability to explain something that already happened.
ArkeStudio/provider-calls/ Safe Same: recent call records, kept for diagnosis. Arke already drops the oldest itself.

What is never written into a world

No keys

Provider keys live in one file, encrypted, in Arke's home folder — never in a world. That is deliberate and it is checked in the code: the store that holds them is described as sitting outside every world precisely so no export can carry one out. Send someone a world folder and you have sent them no credentials.

No absolute paths

Nothing inside a world names a location on your machine. An artifact card names a file, not a path. A take names a reference by its place within the world. Where a path does appear inside a file it is written with forward slashes, whatever machine wrote it.

This is what makes a world portable: copy the folder to a memory stick, open it on a different computer with a different user name and a different drive letter, and every sheet, canon entry, take, version and link still resolves. There is a test that opens a world, changes something, then walks every text file in it looking for anything that looks like an absolute path, and fails if it finds one.

How a file is written safely

A reader must never see half a file. So Arke never writes over a file in place.

  1. Write it alongside, under a temporary name

    The new content goes to a fresh file in the same folder, with a scratch name nothing else will ever look at.

  2. Push it all the way to the disk

    Not just handed to the operating system — flushed, so the bytes are really on the disk before anything else happens. Pulling the plug at this moment costs you the temporary file and nothing else.

  3. Rename it over the target

    A rename is the one thing the filesystem does completely or not at all. After it, the file is wholly new; before it, wholly old. There is no in-between for a reader to catch. If the rename fails, the temporary file is cleaned up and the old file is untouched.

  4. Try again if something is holding the file

    Antivirus and the system's search indexer briefly hold files inside a user folder, and a rename onto a held file fails for a moment and then works. Arke retries with a short backoff rather than reporting a failure that was never real.

A single file is one thing; accepting a change is usually several — the new file, its history snapshot, the world's revision counter, a line in the change log. Those have to land together or not at all, so Arke writes down what it is about to do first, does it, then removes the note. If the power goes at any point, opening the world reads the note and either finishes the change or rolls it back before anything is allowed to read. The detail is in the accept gate.

The one honest gap: some media is copied or moved before its record is written, and those two steps are not a single transaction. An interruption between them can leave a picture in the world with no card beside it. It is an orphan file, not a damaged world.

Long paths, and moving between machines

Windows has an old limit of 260 characters on a path, and a world can nest fairly deep — a world name, then a production name, then a takes folder, then one take's own folder, then a file. Arke asks the operating system for the long-path treatment on every read and write it makes, so its own work is never subject to that limit. It also works out the worst case at first run and warns you if your folder sits somewhere deep enough to trouble other programs — your file explorer, your text editor — which may not have asked for the same treatment. Keeping Arke's home near the top of a drive avoids the whole question.

Separately: any path stored inside a file is written with forward slashes, never backslashes. Windows accepts both, and everything else accepts only one. Writing the one both understand is why a world folder made on Windows opens unchanged elsewhere.

Editing a world by hand

The files are yours and a text editor can open all of them. What happens next depends on whether Arke is running.

While Arke is open

One file is genuinely invited: the bible. Arke watches for it moving, quietly re-reads it, and carries on. That is the feature working, not an anomaly — it is a notes file, and asking you to approve your own typing would be a toll on thinking out loud.

Everything else is a different matter. Arke does not merge your edit and it does not silently adopt it. Worse, it does not reliably notice it either: it is holding its own picture of the world in memory, and the next thing it writes is calculated from that picture, not from your file. Your edit can be quietly written over. Nothing is corrupted, but nothing is safe either. Close the world first.

While Arke is closed

This is the supported way, and it is handled properly. Arke keeps a fingerprint of every file as it stood when it last had the world open. On opening, it compares them and collects what changed: files edited, files that appeared, files that vanished. It does not adopt any of it on its own — each one is surfaced for you to reconcile deliberately.

When you do reconcile an edited sheet, canon entry or chapter, Arke takes the bytes you typed as the new content, cuts the next version, writes the history snapshot, and logs the change as having come from outside the app. A file you deleted stays deleted and gets a line in the log saying so. Anything it does not version — an image, a data file — gets a log line and nothing more.

If the search cache is missing — you deleted it, or the world came from someone else — there is no fingerprint list to compare against, so the world is simply adopted as it stands rather than reported as edited. That is on purpose: a deletable cache must never cost you anything, including a false alarm.
