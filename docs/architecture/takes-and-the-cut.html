Takes and the cut

One result, one folder, and the choice kept somewhere else

Authored things — sheets, canon, scenes — change only when you accept a change to them. Choosing between generated results is a different kind of act, so it is stored in a different place. That split is the most important shape in the product, and most of the rest follows from it.

What a take is

A take is one generated result: one attempt at one shot. It gets its own folder inside the production, its own media file, and a full record of what produced it.

productions\saltlight\ scenes\ 04-the-verse-rises.json the shots you wrote, in order takes\ one folder per result — nothing here is ever rewritten tk_01J8A...A1\ take.json how it was made frame.png what came back tk_01J8C...C3\ an earlier attempt at the same shot — rejected, still here tk_01J8F...B2\ the one currently in the cut selections.json which take each shot is using right now reviews.jsonl every accept and reject you have made cut.json audio tracks and placed clips only

Real names from the sample world, shortened in the middle. Identifiers sort by the moment they were made, so a folder listing is already a history.

coversShots
Which shots this is footage for. Usually one; a whole-scene pass covers several. A reference take carries an empty list, because it covers a character or a place rather than a shot.
provenance
The canon revision and the version of every sheet the prompt drew on, frozen at dispatch. When Maren's sheet later advances to 5 this still says 4 — which is how "these were made before the coat changed" stays answerable.
references, startFrame
The pictures attached, and the still this clip was seeded from when it was chained off the shot before it. Both name places inside the world, never somewhere on your disk.
params
Everything else the dispatch carried, kept as "all that is not already a field of its own" — so a control a provider adds next month is recorded the first day it is used.
cost
Estimated, actual, and where the figure came from. See generation & spend.

A take is never edited

take.json is written once, on arrival, and never again. Try the shot again and you get another take; the first one stays, media and record intact.

That buys three things. A rejected take can still explain what arrived, because nothing overwrote it with your opinion of it. A shot can hold several candidates and a decision between them, rather than collapsing both into one word. And nothing that assembles your film can read a status off a take and become a second answer to what the film is.

A whole-scene pass, and why segments are ranges

Ask for a whole scene at once and one clip comes back covering three shots. Each shot still needs its own reviewable result, so Arke writes three more takes holding no media of their own. Each names the pass and a start and end point inside it.

a segment take: a range into another take's file "segment": { "passTakeId": "tk_01J8G...P1", "inSec": 12, "outSec": 18 }

Cutting real files would mean choosing between two bad options. Re-encoding spends time and picture quality on material you may reject in ten seconds. Copying without re-encoding is lossless but can only cut where the file already has a clean break, which on a six-second clip can land a second from where the shot ends — and a shot that starts a second late is a worse artefact than one never cut at all.

The boundaries come from the plan, not from the clip. They are the numbers worked out before the job was sent. Deciding them afterwards, by inspecting the footage for where a cut seems to be, would be guessing at cuts the model may never have made, then presenting the guess as your scene's structure.

Continuing, rather than restarting

A take may record that it was made by extending an earlier one. Arke allows one hop and refuses a second, so no chain can form in which reselecting one shot invalidates a whole scene's tail. Change a selection something was built on and the extending take keeps its media and record, but the shot using it has its selection cleared — a visible hole, rather than footage the record says no longer applies.

designed The extension link is part of the file's shape and the refusal logic reads it, but nothing that writes an arriving take fills it in yet. The one-hop rule is enforced against a field that is currently always empty.

Reference takes

Not every result is footage. A character sheet, a look, a main photograph, an accepted angle on a location — all takes, with the same record and the same review. They cover a sheet rather than a shot, so their covered-shots list is empty, and they live under references\ beside the character. See the world.

What was measured afterwards lives beside the take

How long a clip actually runs is not something the dispatch knew; a measurement found it out later. So it goes in its own small file in the take's folder, and the take is left alone.

productions\saltlight\takes\tk_01J8F...B2\media-info.json { "sourceHash": "sha256:…", "mediaInfo": { "durationSec": 6.04, "hasAudio": true }, "probedAt": "2026-07-30T14:02:11Z" }

Putting it inside the take would rewrite paid history with something that happened afterwards, and would make "we measured and it failed" indistinguishable from "nobody has measured this yet".

The motion check, and what its absence means

A video take may carry one advisory measurement taken the moment it landed: some providers return a clip claiming a normal frame rate that in fact repeats frames — nominally smooth, effectively half the motion. It is deliberately powerless, because it cannot tell a provider's stutter from a shot you meant to be motionless, so it never rejects, repairs or hides anything. And absent means "not measured", never "measured clean". Older takes are not filled in retroactively: a take is immutable, and inventing a measurement nobody took would be worse than saying nothing.

Where the choice lives

Which take a shot is currently using is written in a separate small file, and nowhere else.

productions\saltlight\selections.json { "sh_12": { "acceptedTakeId": "tk_01J8F0000000000000000000B2", "startFrameTakeId": "tk_01J8A0000000000000000000A1" } }

acceptedTakeId is the take this shot uses. startFrameTakeId is the take whose closing picture the next shot opens on, pinned when you accepted this one. Where the accepted take is a pass segment the frame comes from the pass rather than the segment, so a boundary landing in both does not chain the same picture twice.

This looks like a small filing decision and is the hinge of the whole design. Sheets, canon and scenes are authored: they change only when you accept a change to them, which means a proposal, a check on what else it disturbs, and your decision — the apparatus described in the accept gate. Picking a take is not that kind of act. It is you choosing between results that already exist.

If the choice lived inside the scene file, accepting a take would edit an authored thing, which forces one of two bad outcomes. Either every accepted take drags a proposal and a consequence check behind it, making a pass through forty takes unbearable — or the gate quietly acquires an exception, and the promise that nothing authored changes without your say-so stops being true. Worse, the line between "what I have decided is true" and "what I am currently using" would disappear, and it is that line which lets you change your mind cheaply.

So the scene file stays purely what you wrote, and the selections file stays a small, reversible note about what is in play. Accepting a take creates no proposal and bumps no scene version. How far into the footage a shot starts sits here too, and resets to zero when you pick a different take: 4.2 seconds into one clip is not 4.2 seconds into another.

The record of your judgement

Beside the selections sits a file that only ever grows: one line per decision, in the order you made them.

productions\saltlight\reviews.jsonl {"ts":"2026-07-30T13:58:02Z","takeId":"tk_01J8C0000000000000000000C3","shotId":"sh_12", "decision":"reject","by":"user", "citation":{"sheet":"maren-kest","field":"appearance","note":"coat drifted off-sheet"}} {"ts":"2026-07-30T14:04:11Z","takeId":"tk_01J8F0000000000000000000B2","shotId":"sh_12", "decision":"accept","by":"user"}

Two real lines from the sample world, wrapped to fit. Six minutes apart: a rejection saying exactly what was wrong, then the take that replaced it.

Nothing rewrites an earlier line; a later decision supersedes an earlier one without erasing it. A take nobody has judged is simply one with no line — unreviewed is the absence of a record rather than a flag that could be wrong.

A rejection must cite a sheet and a field; Arke refuses one without both. That turns a shrug into evidence: not "no", but "her coat drifted from what her appearance field says". It leaves the take untouched, leaves any existing selection untouched, and changes nothing about the shot's prompt.

designed The citation is a durable record only. It is collected in the shape that would let rejections feed back into a prompt; nothing reads them that way yet.

Keeping the history rather than only its conclusion is what makes a production explicable to you later. "Which take is in the cut" is answered by the selections file; "why did I go round three times on shot 12" can only be answered by a record nothing overwrote. It is also why the cut needs no versions of its own — going back to an earlier cut means going back to the selections that produced it.

Accepting writes both files in one commit. Split into two writes, a crash between them would leave a decision recorded with no selection set.

The cutderived on every view and every export

Arke walks the scenes in order, walks the shots inside them, and asks the selections file what each shot is using. That is the film. A stored sequence would be a second answer to what the film is, and the two would diverge the first time you changed your mind.

A shot with no chosen take is a gap

It is drawn and counted as one — "13 of 15 shots placed · 2 gaps", and beside it "2 gaps · 30s uncovered". Not an error and not a warning: remaining work, in the right places, at the right lengths. Watching a half-finished film with its holes where the holes actually are is the point of the whole arrangement.

What cut.json is for

Only what has nowhere else to live. First, audio: dialogue, score and ambience tracks. A dialogue entry names the speaking character, the voice take, and the version of that character's sheet the voice was assigned at — two lines from one character can legitimately sound different if they were made either side of a voice change, and that field lets the record say so.

Second, clips you place by hand: a plate, a title card, a piece of music dropped onto the cut at a window you choose. That placement is the one position Arke stores, which is why placed clips sit on lanes of their own — the picture stays derived, so there is still exactly one answer to where a shot sits. A lane is stacking order, as in any editing application: a higher lane is nearer the viewer. Sound ignores lanes, because a mix has no stacking.

A placed clip only ever cites a file, and carries no prompt, no cost and no review, because nothing about it was generated or judged. Its sound can be kept, muted, or split onto the lane below. Deleting the placement removes the placement; the file stays in your world.

When the song is the clock

A music video is not cut by laying shots end to end. The song is fixed before a single frame exists, and every shot has a place in it. For those productions Arke keeps a spine: a master track, and a map of where each shot sits against it. One file, copied nowhere else — two records of one timeline disagree eventually, and the disagreement shows up as a cut that plays correctly for its maker and nobody else.

the track
Which file in your world is the master. Its length is measured, never typed.
markers
Sections carve the song up — "Verse 1" — and lyric markers say what is being sung at a moment, so a shot can be anchored to a line rather than a stopwatch. An imported lyric sheet is read exactly or refused wholesale with the offending line number: half a sheet is worse than none, because the markers you kept would be whichever the file listed first.
anchors
One per shot: the window in the song it occupies, and whether that clip's own sound is kept or muted under the track.

With a spine, Arke walks the song from zero to its end and asks what covers each moment, rather than adding up shot lengths. Everything uncovered comes out as black. A cut with eleven seconds of black in the second verse is a true statement about where the work is; a cut that quietly runs eleven seconds short is not, and it is the one nobody notices until the export is playing next to the song.

Intended length, and measured length

A shot's intended duration is what you wrote when you wrote the shot — six seconds in the scene file. In an ordinary production that is its slot in the cut, and a shot that never stated one is given four. With a spine the anchor's window is the budget instead, and anchoring never rewrites the duration you authored.

A take's measured duration is what the file turned out to be. If it runs shorter than its window, the shortfall becomes a labelled card saying how many seconds are missing — not a frozen last frame, because a frozen frame reads as a creative choice and this is unfinished work.

When the length has never been measured, Arke states it rather than guessing around it. The clip is laid in for the whole window, because that is the only guess producing a watchable cut, and the guess is written down against that shot: "has not been probed, so its length is assumed to cover the window". A review cut may be in that state; a master may not.

What leaves the folder

An export is a single local render of the cut Arke has just worked out. No provider is called and nothing is uploaded. The file lands in your world's own exports\ folder, named for the production, the preset and the moment.

disagrees The master specification's folder diagram puts exports\ inside the production. The code writes it at the top of the world. The code is what runs.

PresetSizeWhat it is for

The difference between the first two is a real decision. A review cut renders whatever there is, because seeing the gaps is the point of watching one. A master claims the film is finished, so it is refused while a shot has no picture, while two shots claim the same seconds, while a clip's length was never measured, or while a shot you wrote is anchored nowhere. The refusal names its reason and arrives before the encode — rather than after somebody has sent the file on.

Gaps become slates: a black card with the shot's label and duration drawn across it, in the same single encode as everything else. Placed clips are laid over the picture, each confined to its own window, their sound delayed to where it was placed.

ffmpeg is a separate program, not part of Arke

Everything touching video frames — encoding an export, cutting a boundary still, measuring a clip — is done by handing arguments to ffmpeg, a long-standing open-source tool Arke runs as a separate program. It is bundled with the packaged desktop build and absent from most other ways of running the application.

So Arke works without it, and says so. Measurements are absent; the motion check is absent; and an export does not fail silently or leave a broken file — it states its reason: "export needs ffmpeg — bundled in packaged builds; set ARKE_FFMPEG to use one now". A spine production whose track cannot be measured refuses the same way, rather than exporting as though it had no track at all.

The encode is written to a hidden staging file and moved into exports\ only when it finishes; cancel it and the staging file is deleted. There is never a half-rendered film looking like a finished one. Exporting the whole world is a different act — see files on disk.

The rules on this page

RuleWhat it means for you
A take is never edited Every result you generated is still there, with the record of exactly how it was made. Trying again costs you nothing you already had.
The choice is stored separately Accepting a take changes one small operational file. It creates no proposal, bumps no version, and touches nothing you authored.
Reviews are append-only You keep the history of your judgement, not just its conclusion — including what was wrong with the takes you turned down.
The cut is derived No stored edit can drift out of step with your scenes. Change a selection and the film changes, with nothing to reconcile.
A gap is visible work A shot with no chosen take is drawn, counted and exported as a labelled hole of the right length — never omitted, never an error.
Absent means not measured A missing duration or a missing motion check means nobody looked, never that everything is fine. Arke says which.
