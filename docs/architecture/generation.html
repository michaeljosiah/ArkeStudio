Generation and spend

Pressing the button

Everything Arke makes for you — a still, a six-second clip, a line of dialogue in a character's voice — travels down one road. The same road whether the work runs on a service you pay by the second or on the graphics card in your own machine.

The unit is a dispatch: one request, sent once, for one result. Arke calls the record of it a job, and a job is not a button press. It is a durable line in a file that outlives the press, the window, and the machine being switched off mid-render.

What actually happens

  1. The request is assembled

    Not from the shot alone: the shot's action, the world's look, the scene's place and time of day, and the model sheets of whoever is in it. A character whose picture is going to travel contributes the picture; one whose picture is not going contributes their written description instead, so nobody in the shot goes undescribed.

  2. It is written down before it is sent

    A line is appended to queue/jobs.jsonl saying the job exists, what it is for, which model it goes to and what Arke expects it to cost. Then a second line, saying Arke is about to send it. Only then does anything leave the machine.

  3. It goes to the provider

    With an errand number — a one-off identifier written down before the request. Where a service honours one, it recognises a repeat of the same errand and refuses to do the work twice.

  4. The result is checked before it is kept

    The returned file lands in a hidden staging folder, is inspected to confirm it is really the kind of file it claims and arrived whole, and only then moves into your world under its final name. If a job returns several files and one fails, none lands.

  5. The take and its charge are recorded

    The final state goes to the job log and exactly one charge line goes to ledger.jsonl. Then the result becomes a take — what a take is, and how it reaches your cut, belongs to takes & the cut.

Written down before it happens, never after

There are two ways a program that spends your money can betray you, and they are opposites. It can lose work you paid for, or pay twice for work you asked for once. A queue that only records what it intends to do prevents the first and causes the second: after a crash an unsent request and a sent-but-unwitnessed request look identical, and the obvious recovery — sending it — is the double charge.

So the rule is that every change of state is written down before the action it authorises. If the power goes the instant before a request leaves, what remains is a record of something in flight rather than a gap.

Never trust silence

Finding a job that reads submitting on restart, Arke does not know what happened, and does not guess. A gap in what it could observe is unknown, to be found out — never a failure to quietly retry. Guessing "it probably never sent" charges you twice for one clip; guessing "it probably did" throws away work you already paid for. So Arke goes and looks, and how depends on what the service can be asked:

Ask by number
Where a service can be asked "did you receive errand 01J8E1?", the answer is definite both ways: found means Arke adopts the work, not found means it sends safely.
Check the recent list
Where a service can only list recent work, a match is conclusive. Absence is only conclusive if the list reaches back past the moment in question, so Arke checks the oldest entry's age before believing a negative.
Ask you
Where it can do neither, the job is parked and you are asked, with the cost of a possible duplicate in plain money: "Arke did not witness the submission result. fal may have accepted and charged it, and cannot confirm what happened. No automatic retry was made. Resubmitting may charge about $0.13 again; the prior actual cost is unknown." This is not a hole in the design. It is the only honest position when nobody can say what happened.

This runs at start-up and finishes before the queue accepts new work, so coming back to forty jobs you are told what Arke found rather than handed a queue that resumed on assumptions. A job that already carries the service's own reference number is the exception: it resumes by watching, never by resending.

What a job line says

queue/jobs.jsonl lives at the application level, not inside a world, because jobs from every open world compete for one account's rate limit and one machine's graphics card. It is append-only: a job is never edited, it gains a new complete line each time it changes. Keep the last line for each job and you have the present; read them in order and you have the history.

queue\jobs.jsonl {"id":"jb_01J8E0000000000000000000J2", "idempotencyKey":"01J8E1000000000000000000K2", "worldId":"01J8F3K2QW9VZX4N7M0RTYB6HC", "productionId":"saltlight", "target":{"kind":"shot","id":"sh_12","coversShots":["sh_12"]}, "provider":"fal", "model":"seedance-2.0", "capability":"video", "params":{"aspect":"16:9","durationSec":6}, "estimatedMicroUsd":130000, "status":"succeeded", "providerJobId":"fal_q3c", "attempt":1, "error":null, "createdAt":"2026-07-30T14:01:05Z", "updatedAt":"2026-07-30T14:02:04Z"}

A real line from the sample world, wrapped to fit. Nothing in it is a secret or a path outside the world.

status
One of seven words: queued, submitting, running, succeeded, failed, cancelled, and needs-reconciliation — the one meaning Arke could not observe what happened and is waiting on you.
target
What the job is for, in the caller's own words: a shot, a whole scene pass, a model sheet, a voice line, a reference tile. The queue does not know what any of those mean, which is why one queue serves all of them.
provider, model
Which service, and which specific model on it — never just "a video provider", because a default that resolved only to a company would give you a different price and different limits on different days.
estimatedMicroUsd
What Arke expected this to cost, in millionths of a dollar, worked out before the request left. 130000 is thirteen cents.
attempt
How many physical sends have been authorised, written down before each one. A crash may make Arke overcount by one; it can never undercount, which is the direction that matters when a send costs money.
deletedAt
Removing a finished job from your Activity list appends a final line marking it gone rather than cutting a line out. Its charge line is untouched: what was spent stays spent.

A reconcile action

At start-up Arke works through every unfinished job and reports what it did to each. That report entry is a reconcile action, and there are seven possible answers: adopted (the work was found and is now being watched), resubmitted (it was provably never received), resumed polling (it had a reference number all along), requeued, failed, ledger-completed (the job had finished but its charge line was missing, so it was written), and held for user.

When something goes wrong

Retrying the wrong thing is expensive in a way that retrying an ordinary web page is not: a content refusal retried five times is five charges for the same refusal. So failures are sorted into four kinds, and each behaves differently.

KindLooks likeWhat Arke does
Too fast, or a hiccup Rate limit, server error, timeout, dropped connection Waits and tries again, up to four sends. Each wait is roughly double the last with a random fraction shaved off, capped at thirty seconds. A rate limit also slows that service's whole lane.
Refused Content policy, invalid settings, a model that does not exist No retry. The reason is shown. Sending it again buys the same refusal.
Your account Rejected or revoked key, quota gone, billing failed That service's lane pauses and you are told once. Queued jobs stay queued rather than failing one by one — they were never wrong, the credential was. A bad FAL key does not stop ElevenLabs.
Offline No network at all Jobs stay queued. Closing a laptop mid-scene and reopening it elsewhere should give you a queue that carries on, not a screen of errors.

An error Arke cannot recognise is treated as refused: failing visibly is cheaper than retrying wrongly.

Checking the file before keeping it

Arke reads the first bytes of a returned file to confirm it is what it claims and, for the formats that have one, reads the end to confirm the closing marker is there — that is what a cut-off download loses. An empty download is refused outright. For pictures the bytes, not the service's word, decide whether it is really a PNG, a JPEG or a WebP, and the landed file is named accordingly.

Why an unverified file is worse than a failure: a clip cut off in transit still plays. It plays for two seconds and stops. You watch it, decide the model got the shot wrong, and generate again — paying a second time for a problem that was never the model's. A failed job costs you one look at an error message. A half-downloaded file lies to you and costs you another generation.

What gets to come along

A shot with four characters and a location cites five reference pictures. The model you chose may accept four. Some accept one. Some accept none — all three of Arke's own local recipes accept none. Something has to give, and the decision cannot be silent: a quiet cap reads on screen as full coverage, and you end up blaming the model for drift that a rule you never saw actually caused.

So Arke ranks the candidates in a fixed order, takes what fits, and names what it dropped before you commit.

Four things can run out, and Arke names which one bound the request, because they behave differently:

Ran out ofBehaviourWhy

A cited character with no picture on file consumes no slot — their written description still travels in the words. And a model that accepts no pictures says so rather than silently sending none: "Local · Draft Image accepts no reference images — those images are omitted; only existing sheet descriptions remain for maren-kest, bray-half-hitch."

Money

Every amount in Arke is a whole number of millionths of a dollar, rounded exactly once, at the moment a figure goes on screen. Prices reach fractions of a cent and a scene is dozens of them added up; a total assembled from decimals accumulates error you cannot see and cannot reconcile against a real invoice.

Knowing before you spend

Arke ships with a price list — the model manifest — covering every model it can dispatch to. It is a plain file inside the application, read at start-up and never fetched over the network: a product that works offline should not need the internet to tell you what a button costs. Video bills by the second and often differently per resolution, images per picture or per megapixel, speech per character; the list carries each shape as its own kind rather than flattening them, so an estimate is arithmetic rather than a guess. A model absent from the list is refused, not attempted — dispatching without an estimate would break the app's one promise about money.

The ledger

ledger.jsonl sits beside the job log at the application level. One line per finished job. Append-only, never rewritten, never compacted; the only repair it permits is trimming a final line left half-written by a crash, which removes nothing complete.

ledger.jsonl {"ts":"2026-07-30T14:02:04Z", "worldId":"01J8F3K2QW9VZX4N7M0RTYB6HC", "productionId":"saltlight", "jobId":"jb_01J8E0000000000000000000J2", "provider":"fal", "model":"seedance-2.0", "outcome":"succeeded", "estimatedMicroUsd":130000, "actualMicroUsd":128400, "actualSource":"provider-reported"}

Failures and cancellations get a line too. Arke cannot promise you were not billed for a job that failed, and cancelling after work has begun does not reliably prevent the charge. A ledger that omitted them would drift from your real invoice in exactly the direction that destroys trust in it.

actualSource is the field that keeps it honest. provider-reported means the service said what it charged; manifest-derived means nobody said anything, so the shipped estimate was recorded as the actual; local-zero means it ran on your own machine. A total mixing measured figures with derived ones is not a measured total, and Arke labels it as mixed.

The ledger keys on the world's permanent identifier — the long code in worldId — never on its folder name or its title. Rename a world and its whole spending history follows, because none of it was ever attached to a name. Renaming does not move the folder either: the folder is an address, the title is a word you should be free to rewrite.

Drift, and the rolling figure

A hand-written price list goes stale as providers change prices. Arke does not prevent that; it makes it visible. Because every line holds both the expectation and the charge, the gap is measurable: when one model's charges have diverged from its shipped price by more than fifteen per cent across at least three jobs, it is named on the spend screen with the size of the difference. Only genuinely reported charges count — comparing an estimate against an estimate would only measure Arke's own arithmetic.

The figure Arke shows you is a rolling one: not a calendar month and not a running total since you installed it, but everything spent in the last N days counted backwards from now, seven by default. Tomorrow, today's spend is still in it and last Monday's has fallen out. It answers "what is this costing me lately", and it does not reset on the first of the month and flatter you.

It counts across all your worlds, because one key produces one bill however many worlds it was spent from. Cross a threshold you set and Arke tells you once, not on every job after. It alerts and never blocks: a hard stop mid-scene with a cut half assembled is worse than a warning, and the money is yours, on your own account.

Where your keys live

credentials.dat, at the application level beside the job log and the ledger — never inside a world. Each key is encrypted against a secret your operating system holds for your account, so the file is useless on another machine or under another login even if copied, and every write resets its permissions so only your account can read it.

Worlds are meant to be copied, zipped and handed to a collaborator. A key inside one would mean sharing your work shares your account, so sharing a world never shares a credential. The unencrypted key exists only in the memory of the running application, and is registered with the redaction boundary the moment you type it — anything that later tries to write it to a log finds it already scrubbed, rather than every logging call site being expected to remember.

The one that is not Arke's to hold

Higgsfield is different. Arke does not talk to it over the web at all; it drives Higgsfield's own command-line tool, which signs in for itself. That sign-in is a browser flow: Arke starts the tool, the tool opens your browser, you sign in there, and the tool keeps its own short-lived token. Arke waits — up to five minutes, then stops and says so, because a login left open forever is a stuck screen.

Nothing is copied out of that tool into credentials.dat: a second copy of a token the tool refreshes on its own schedule would be stale by design. So "is Higgsfield set up" is answered by asking the tool, not by looking for a file, and the answer can change while the app is running. If your sign-in reaches several billing accounts and none is picked, Arke says so before dispatching anything — a generation billed to the wrong account cannot be taken back.

The record of what was sent

provider-calls/calls.jsonl holds a redacted copy of every physical call Arke makes on your behalf, so that when something looks wrong you can see what was actually sent instead of reconstructing it from memory.

scrubbed
Any field whose name looks like a secret is replaced outright, every key Arke knows is scrubbed from the text, and the signed tail of every web address is cut off — those carry temporary access tokens.
yours only
Restricted to your account, like the credential file, and deliberately left out of the support bundle Arke can package for a bug report.
bounded
Past two thousand records or fifty megabytes it is compacted: the newest two thousand are rewritten and the rest dropped. A debugging aid that grows without limit is a disk problem you did not ask for.
both kinds
Web calls and command-line calls share one record, because they answer the same question. Neither is dressed as the other: an exit code is not a web status, and mapping one to the other would put a number in your history no service ever returned.

Generating on your own machine

Local generation exists so the iteration a shot goes through before it is worth paying for does not accrue per-second charges. Arke never exposes ComfyUI as such: what you get is a short menu of Arke-written recipes, sitting in the same picker as the paid models, chosen the same way, queued the same way, landing takes the same way, and written to the ledger the same way — at zero, labelled unmetered.

Local · Draft Image

Stills at 1024 across. Needs about 6 GB of graphics memory.

Local · Draft Video

Two, three or five seconds, wide or upright. Needs about 8 GB.

Local · Cloned Voice

Speech in a voice made from a recording that never leaves the machine. Needs about 8 GB.

A recipe is a fixed thing, not a workshop: no graph editor, no importing someone else's workflow. It is also a promise about what it will need, so each names the exact model files and add-ons it depends on, down to a fingerprint of the contents, checked immediately before every dispatch. A file that does not match what the recipe pinned switches the recipe off with that as the reason, rather than producing a picture from something else.

Speech and dictation run separately, through a small supervised helper Arke starts on your machine that listens only to itself: it reads text aloud in preset voices and transcribes push-to-talk dictation into any chat box. Cloud speech does not go through it — that takes the ordinary road, through the queue, with an errand number and a ledger line, so there is exactly one path where money is concerned.

Readiness, checked before you press anything

A text model degrades gracefully on a small machine. A video model that does not fit in graphics memory does not run at all. So Arke checks in order: is there an engine, does it answer, is it new enough, does it have the add-on this recipe needs, are the model files present, do they match their fingerprints, is the card big enough. The first failure switches the recipe off with that reason named — and the queue enforces the same answer on the way in, so nothing is written down for a dispatch the app already knows cannot run.

Two judgement calls sit inside that. A card big enough overall but mostly held by another program right now gets a different sentence from one that is simply too small — one will never work, the other is busy. And a card that could not be measured at all reads unknown and dispatches anyway: refusing on a machine that would have worked costs you the feature, while allowing one that then refuses in a quarter of a second costs you a click.

Missing is a state, not a bug

Arke assumes almost nothing about your machine. What is absent is reported with the reason, and the rest of the application carries on. Three real examples:

no encoder No video tooling

A standard media encoder draws the still that represents a clip, measures whether a clip actually moves, and exports a finished cut. It ships inside the packaged application and is absent almost everywhere else. Without it, clips still generate, land and can be watched and accepted — they simply have no thumbnail and no motion measurement. Only export refuses, and it says why. The split is deliberate: a thumbnail is drawn while a paid take is being filed, and an error thrown there would destroy a generation you had already paid for.

no card Not enough graphics memory

A local recipe your machine cannot run is listed and switched off, with both figures and a way forward: "Needs 8 GB VRAM. This machine has 6 GB. Cloud video still works." Listed, not hidden — a menu that quietly omits things leaves you wondering what you are missing.

no key No credential stored

A job for a service with no key does not fail. Its lane pauses with the reason "no credential stored for this provider", the job returns to the front of the queue, and other services keep working. And if Higgsfield's tool is not installed at all, every call returns "the Higgsfield CLI is not installed on this machine" — the remedy — rather than an obscure error that reads as though your shot failed to render.

The speech helper follows the same pattern. Not running, it names what is lost: "Voxa is not running — local voice and dictation are off; cloud voice still works."

What is written down when you spend money

Four things, in three places, none of which is a database. Arke keeps a search index to make screens fast, and that index is a cache: delete it and it rebuilds from the files below, every figure identical.

WrittenWhereWhat it holdsSurvives deleting the index
The job queue/jobs.jsonl Every state the job passed through, in order: what was asked for, what it was expected to cost, how many sends were authorised, how it ended. yes
The charge ledger.jsonl One line per finished job, failures and cancellations included, with the estimate and the actual kept apart and the source of the actual named. yes
The call provider-calls/calls.jsonl What was actually sent and what came back, secrets scrubbed, bounded in size, readable only by your account. yes
The take inside the world The media file itself and the record of what produced it. See takes & the cut. yes

All four survive, because in every case the log is the truth and the index is the convenience. The job log and the ledger sit outside every world by design: a world's index can be deleted, a world can be renamed, a world can be moved to another machine, and none of it touches what you spent or what you asked for. If you ever want to check Arke's arithmetic, you can open the file.
