Generation and spend

Pressing the button

Everything Arke makes for you — a still, a six-second clip, a line of dialogue in a character's voice — travels down one road. The same road whether the work runs on a service you pay by the second or on the graphics card in your own machine.

The unit is a dispatch: one request, sent once, for one result. Arke calls the record of it a job, and a job is not a button press. It is a durable line in a file that outlives the press, the window, and the machine being switched off mid-render.

What actually happens

  1. The request is assembled

    Not from the shot alone: the shot's action, the world's look, the scene's place and time of day, and the model sheets of whoever is in it. A character whose picture is going to travel contributes the picture; one whose picture is not going contributes their written description instead, so nobody in the shot goes undescribed. The world's look is stated once at the top and restated once at the bottom as what must not drift — never per beat, because a model told the same thing four times spends its attention arguing about which mention is authoritative.

  2. It is written down before it is sent

    A line is appended to queue/jobs.jsonl saying the job exists, what it is for, which model it goes to and what Arke expects it to cost. Then a second line, saying Arke is about to send it. Only then does anything leave the machine.

  3. It goes to the provider

    With an errand number — a one-off identifier written down before the request. Where a service honours one, it recognises a repeat of the same errand and refuses to do the work twice. Jobs queue per service in the order you asked, at a rate Arke lowers by itself if the service says you are going too fast.

  4. The result is checked before it is kept

    The returned file lands in a hidden staging folder, is inspected to confirm it is really the kind of file it claims and arrived whole, and only then moves into your world under its final name. If a job returns several files and one fails, none lands.

  5. The take and its charge are recorded

    The final state goes to the job log and exactly one charge line goes to ledger.jsonl. Then the result becomes a take — what a take is, and how it reaches your cut, belongs to takes & the cut.

Written down before it happens, never after

There are two ways a program that spends your money can betray you, and they are opposites. It can lose work you paid for, or pay twice for work you asked for once. A queue that only records what it intends to do prevents the first and causes the second: after a crash an unsent request and a sent-but-unwitnessed request look identical, and the obvious recovery — sending it — is the double charge.

So the rule is that every change of state is written down before the action it authorises. The instant before a request leaves, the job log already says one is leaving. If the power goes then, what remains is a record of something in flight rather than a gap.

Never trust silence

The second half matters more. Finding a job that reads submitting on restart, Arke does not know what happened, and does not guess. A gap in what it could observe is unknown, to be found out — never a failure to quietly retry.

A retry is not free. Guessing "it probably never sent" charges you twice for one clip; guessing "it probably did" throws away work you already paid for. So Arke goes and looks, and how it looks depends on what the service can be asked:

Ask by number
Where a service can be asked "did you receive errand 01J8E1?", the answer is definite both ways: found means Arke adopts the work, not found means it sends safely.
Check the recent list
Where a service can only list recent work, a match is conclusive. Absence is only conclusive if the list reaches back past the moment in question — so Arke checks the oldest entry's age before believing a negative.
Ask you
Where a service can do neither, the job is parked and you are asked, with the cost of a possible duplicate in plain money: "Arke did not witness the submission result. fal may have accepted and charged it, and cannot confirm what happened. No automatic retry was made. Resubmitting may charge about $0.13 again; the prior actual cost is unknown." This is not a hole in the design. It is the only honest position when nobody can say what happened.

Reconciliation runs at start-up and finishes before the queue accepts new work. Coming back to forty jobs, you are told what Arke found rather than handed a queue that resumed on assumptions. A job that already carries the service's own reference number is the one exception: it resumes by watching, never by resending.

The assistant

It drafts with you, and it decides nothing

Arke has a writing assistant. It reads your world, argues with you about it, and writes drafts. What is worth explaining is not what it can do — it is the fence around it: what it may see, what it may touch, and the fact that everything it makes still has to come to you.

There are two kinds of conversation, and they differ only in what they already know.

Talking about the world

Open-ended, about a story. You describe what you have been thinking; it replies like a collaborator and keeps a running account of what it understood — a list of propositions: this character, this rule, this open question.

Talking about one thing

A thread opened from something you were looking at — an entry, a sheet, a production, an episode, a scene. It starts knowing the subject, so you never describe what is already on the screen.

A scene thread is not a blank page. This is the real orientation Arke writes for one, built from the scene as it stands:

the opening lines a scene thread is given This is the scene thread for "The verse rises" (sc_04) in the production saltlight. Its script is ordered blocks that shots cite; propose the whole block list as it should read, keeping an existing block's id when only its text changes. Read the season and the episode this scene serves before deciding against them. A shot itself can be settled here: propose the shot to amend, or leave the shot out to add one at the end. Carry only the fields that change. A shot's id and its number are not yours to set, and reordering is the storyboard's drag, not a proposition.

What a conversation produces

A proposal. Never a direct edit. Nothing you type and nothing it writes creates, accepts, discards or dispatches anything — not "yes", not "do it", not "make it so". Two actions cross that line and both are buttons: wrapping up a conversation, and accepting one of the proposals it made.

Agreement in conversation is cheap and easy to misread, so it is worth nothing here. The assistant is told never to say a change has been made, because it never has. See the accept gate for what the buttons do.

Where it works

Its working folder is a scratch folder, never your world

Every program that edits files has a working folder — where it is standing when it writes something without saying where. For Arke's assistant that folder is never your world. It is one of two things.

drafting
The proposal it is working on: a small folder inside your world's .proposals\ holding copies of exactly the files in scope. It edits the copies.
conversation
A fresh folder outside your world entirely, under Arke's own storage, made for one turn and deleted afterwards. It holds that turn's settings and nothing else.
%APP_ROOT%\run\world-chat\<conversation>\<run>\ made per turn, swept at startup

The second is deliberate, and the source says why: a program treats its working folder as somewhere it may write, so a scratch folder inside the world would be a writable path in the one place this feature promises never to write. So anything the assistant writes without naming a location lands in the draft, by construction — not because it was asked nicely, but because that is the only world it was shown.

Stated rather than assumed away. A malfunctioning assistant that got hold of a shell could still damage world files between saves. Arke detects that — every accept compares the file against a fingerprint taken when the draft was made, and opening a world reconciles anything that changed underneath — and keeps the history. It does not prevent it; prevention needs the operating system to hold the boundary, which is deferred. An earlier draft of the design claimed prevention outright and was wrong, which is why this page is worded as carefully as it is.

What it can see

It cannot browse your world. It has to ask.

Its draft folder holds only what it is working on. Everything else — canon, sheets, productions — it has to request, one question at a time, through a single narrow channel Arke runs itself. Six questions when drafting, eight in a conversation. These are the real descriptions it is given, word for word:

search the canon Lexically search the world's canon entries. Returns ranked candidates with FULL statement text so answers can quote verifiable spans, plus how many entries were searched. Below-floor results mean canon does not answer the query. fetch one entry Fetch one canon entry by id: title, status, lineage, full statement. fetch one sheet Fetch one sheet by slug (character, location or faction): frontmatter and prose sections at the current version. list what exists List world entities of a kind, with status and version. what cites what What cites and is cited by an entity id — citations with the versions they were made at. fetch a production Fetch one production's records whole: story, season, episodes with their promises and scene memberships, and the scene index.

A conversation gets two more: searching the sheets, and reading text out of a document you handed it.

Look at what is missing. Nothing here writes. And nowhere is there a place to put a file name, a folder or a path — every question is answered by name or by search terms. A channel that cannot express a location on disk cannot be pointed at one, which is a great deal stronger than asking it not to.

Why this beats handing it the folder

  1. There is no path to get wrong

    A folder invites a wrong turn: a step upward, a shortcut, a path assembled from two half-correct pieces. A question has no such surface.

  2. Every read is observable

    Arke answers the questions, so Arke knows what was asked and what came back. That is what makes the progress lines possible — "checked: CANON-031 · CANON-002 · the Bell Market, no contradiction if memory is passive" is one search, in your language.

  3. It works on worlds too big to read

    A conversation holds only so much text. Ranked search over the world's own index — the list Arke keeps of every word in every entry — does not stop working when the world outgrows it.

What it may claim

Citations, and refusing to guess

Every claim it makes about your world has to say where it read it: which entry, at which version, and a fingerprint of that text as it stood. A real record, from the sample world:

what one search left behind "tool": "search-canon", "status": "complete", "querySummary": "ledger corrections · the fourth hand · Charter Night bells", "consulted": [ { "ref": { "kind": "canon", "entryId": "CANON-050" }, "observedVersion": 62, "contentHash": "sha256:9c41e7b2a05d83f6" }, { "ref": { "kind": "canon", "entryId": "CANON-064" }, "observedVersion": 87, "contentHash": "sha256:77b0c2d94e1a5f38" } ], "searchedCount": 14

The version and the fingerprint are what make a citation checkable later; if the entry has moved on, it no longer matches. Those three values used to go only to Arke, so the assistant was being asked to cite numbers it had never been shown — and every citation it produced was therefore either left out or invented. Now each answer comes back in two parts: the thing you asked for, and a short block naming exactly what may be cited from it.

This is what makes "the canon never guesses" a mechanism rather than a promise. Quotations are checked, mechanically, against the text they claim to come from, and an invented one fails. If any part of the evidence fails, the whole turn is rejected and retried once with the failure named — never patched up by dropping the claim that would not verify. Dropping is the dangerous option: "No, she cannot" plus "unless she has stood in that water before" becomes a confident falsehood the moment the second half is removed.

When the world has no answer there is no model call at all: if nothing comes above a relevance floor, the refusal happens before the assistant gets a chance to improvise. A refusal reports how many entries were searched, shows the closest ones anyway, and offers the two useful exits — open it as an unanswered question, or draft an answer with the results in front of you.

Two smaller rules, both learned the hard way. Only your words count as evidence, never the assistant's own earlier replies, or a guess made two turns ago comes back as a verified fact. And quotations are matched forgivingly about layout and strictly about content: line wrapping and curly quotes are folded, a paragraph break is not — without that boundary a "quotation" could stitch two unrelated sentences from opposite ends of an entry into one claim the source never makes.

Which world

A conversation is tied to the world it started in

When a conversation asks a question it does not ask about "whichever world is open". It asks with a pass, issued when the turn began, naming one world and one conversation, valid for thirty minutes — longer than a turn, far shorter than a session.

Every question compares the pass against the world actually open, and if they disagree the question is refused: never redirected, never quietly answered from the other world. A long turn outlives a moment of screen state, so without the pass, switching worlds mid-turn would have the assistant answer out of the new one. Nothing would look wrong. The answer would be well-formed, the citation would verify, and it would be about the wrong place entirely.

Even the refusals are careful: they say that the world the pass was issued for is no longer the open one, and stop, because naming the world that is open would leak one conversation's subject into another's. Drafting sessions get no pass and need none — they live and die inside the world you are looking at.

Backstops

When it has to ask you, and remembering the answer

Occasionally it reaches for something that needs your say-so. You are asked in Arke's words, not the machine's — "The agent wants to run a shell command" — and you can answer once or answer always. Answering always writes a remembered grant, kept in grants.json at the application level rather than inside your world, so a world you copy elsewhere carries none of your answers with it.

grants.json — the whole shape { "grants": [ { "id": "grant_m4x2q1", "actionClass": "edit", "createdAt": "2026-08-14T09:12:44Z" }, { "id": "grant_m4x9tt", "actionClass": "webfetch", "createdAt": "2026-08-19T18:03:10Z", "revoked": true } ] }

Revoking marks the line revoked rather than deleting it. The grant stops working at once — only unmarked grants count — but the record of what was granted, and when, survives. A permissions list that erases its own history cannot be audited.

These prompts are meant to be rare, and frequency is treated as a bug. In the confined configuration the assistant's only capability is editing files inside the draft you asked for, which is not worth interrupting you about. Prompts appearing often in normal work mean the fence has drifted. The real gate is the accept gate.

Specified but not built. The worldbuilding-chat issue requires that a remembered grant can never override a hard denial, and that a request for a denied capability be rejected automatically and recorded as a configuration defect. In the code, the remembered-grant check compares the action's name and nothing else: there is no cross-check against the permitted list, and nothing records a defect. The denials are enforced earlier, so a denied capability should not reach a prompt at all — but the belt the issue describes is not there beside the braces.

Two engines

Two different assistants behind one door

Arke ships with an assistant built in, and can also drive Claude Code if you already have it installed and signed in. You never talk to either directly: you ask Arke for a draft, and one of them does the drafting.

 The built-in oneYour own Claude Code
How it runs A separate program Arke starts, watches, restarts if it falls over, and stops on quit, leaving nothing behind. Driven directly. It starts its own program; a session lasts only as long as Arke keeps hold of it.
Where it comes from Inside the installer. Works on a brand-new machine with nothing else on it. Yours. Arke never ships a copy — the point is to drive the one you already trust.

The fence itself is written down once, in nobody's vocabulary, as a list of what is permitted rather than a list of what is banned. That was measured, not assumed: ban one shell and an assistant reaches for another to do the same job, and a program that updates itself grows capabilities nobody has heard of. Anything not on the list is refused.

Why the second one is tested rather than trusted

It is somebody else's program, on a version Arke does not control, that updates itself without asking — one moved from 2.1.177 to 2.1.235 in the middle of a single working session. What would break is the fence, and it would break silently, so a version number is not good enough.

So Arke checks. It runs one throwaway job in a temporary folder, asks for something it has just forbidden, then looks at the folder to see whether it happened. Three ways to fail, and only one is dramatic: the forbidden thing happened; the decision was never put to Arke at all, which proves nothing and is therefore a refusal; or the test could not run. Every build tried so far passes, which is why the check has to be cheap enough to always run. The answer is remembered per version, so an update invalidates it.

A measured limit, recorded in the source. Against version 2.1.235, Arke is not consulted about every action: work that changes nothing and stays inside the working folder is approved before the question reaches it. Audited with everything denied, the draft was unchanged, the forbidden file was never created, and a read aimed outside the working folder did reach Arke and was refused. The boundary holds for anything that changes something or leaves the folder; what slips past is searching inside a folder the assistant is already confined to and already allowed to search.

With Claude Code, Arke also never stops to ask you mid-job: an installation advertises around thirty capabilities and gained five between two runs of one experiment, so anything unrecognised is refused outright rather than parked on a prompt nobody is there to see. The plumbing is on the program.

The outside world

Going online, stated exactly

It was a permanent denial: the requirement named network tools alongside the shell as always disabled. That was amended on 23 August 2026. Reaching the web is now a capability governed by a setting that is off until you turn it on, and the requirement's own wording is that with the setting off, network tools are disabled exactly as before. The shell was not amended and stays permanently refused.

The recorded reason is a reason and not an excuse: the blanket denial was never a policy anyone had chosen for research. Asking the assistant to go and look something up produced a refusal no rule had decided and none could lift, and the person who asked saw only an assistant that would not do as it was told. Reading a public page is not a way out of the working folder, which is what the rest of that requirement is about.

With it on, a page you name is fetched and kept as an attachment on the conversation, so a claim taken from the web stays checkable rather than being verified against a live address that reads differently next month. Nothing it returns is canon; it reaches your world through the accept gate like everything else.

Two places where the older, flatter claim survives in writing. The Claude Code feature guide, published four days before the amendment, still lists reaching the internet as never allowed; and the note beside the shipped guidance documents still says flatly that drafting sessions have no network tools. Both were true when written, and are now true only with the setting off — which is the default.

Guidance ships with the application and is never fetched

The first reason is structural rather than principled: an assistant with the network denied could not fetch a document it needed, so a design that fetched them would fail in exactly the configuration that is the default. The second is that first run works on a machine that has never been online. The third is that no outside server sits between you and drafting a scene.

Who does what

Named assistants, and guidance attached to a job

There is not one assistant but several, each with a purpose and a written brief. They belong to the application, not to your world, so a world copied to another machine carries no assistants with it and behaves identically when it arrives.

You may rewrite a brief in settings; you may not rewrite the rules around it. The order on the page is the enforcement — rules first, your brief and any guidance in the middle, the contract the application checks against last. A brief talked out of the rules fails in ways that look like Arke's own bugs, which, before that ordering existed, is what happened.

Guidance attached to a kind of work

Separately from who is doing the work, there is guidance attached to particular jobs — drafting a scene, drawing a storyboard — written for one family of video models, because the same instruction does not land the same way everywhere. A real extract:

drawing a storyboard this model family can read Line art only. Stick figures and simple contour drawing. Flat, clean, uncluttered, no rendering, no shading, no texture, no colour grading. A dense, heavily rendered board reads as noise and produces a still output or panels in the wrong order. No text inside the image. No labels, captions, shot numbers, arrows with words, or titles. Text drawn on a panel is text the model may render into the frame, and a burned-in caption cannot be removed from the footage afterwards.

Guidance can narrow below a family when a family is not of one mind: one model runs to thirty seconds where its predecessor stops at fifteen, and thirty seconds is not fifteen twice but a sequence with movements in it. Advice hedged across both would fit neither, so the narrower document wins for the models it names, and nothing is ever handed another family's advice.

A scene records which guidance it was drafted under, with the version number, as provenance — two scenes drafted either side of a change differ for a reason otherwise unrecoverable. That record does real work later: a dispatch may override which model shoots a scene, so a scene written for one family can be sent to another without any rule being broken. Arke compares the two and says so before money moves — see generation and spend.

On disk

Where a conversation is kept

Inside the world, one folder per conversation. Operational state, like staged proposals — not part of the record of what your world is.

the-undersong\ ├─ .conversations\ │ └─ cv_01J8F3K2QW9VZX4N7M0RTYB6HC\ │ ├─ conversation.json who it is: an id and when it started │ ├─ events.jsonl append-only; the record │ └─ checkpoint.json a summary, rebuilt if missing ├─ .proposals\ where drafts are staged └─ world.json

The list of events is the truth and is only ever added to, one line per thing that happened, in order. The first line of the real sample conversation, trimmed:

{"seq":1,"at":"2026-08-06T21:00:00Z","event":{ "type": "conversation.created", "title": "The fourth hand", "entryContext": { "kind": "canon-question", "question": "Whose hand writes the ledger's corrections?", "candidateEntryIds": ["CANON-050"] }}}

The third file is a summary, kept only so a long conversation reopens quickly. It is never allowed to be believed over the list of events: it is distrusted if it cannot be read, and distrusted if it claims to describe events the list does not contain — which is precisely what a half-finished write leaves behind. Deleting it costs a rebuild and nothing else.

Voice

Speaking to it in build

There is a specification for talking to Arke out loud, and its governing rule is a good one: voice changes the input, it never changes the artefact. A spoken exchange produces exactly the turns, proposals and files a typed one would. You can talk your way to a draft; you cannot talk your way past an accept.

built
Dictation. You speak instead of typing, it is transcribed on your own machine, and the words appear in the composer as editable text that is never sent. The recording is dropped once transcribed. That editing step is the whole safety story: a world is made of exactly the proper nouns transcription gets wrong, and a mis-heard sentence that sent itself would be a proposal nobody meant.
not built
Everything else in that specification — spoken replies, interrupting playback, skipping it, a listening session spanning several turns, choosing the studio's own voice. The realtime channel it would run over is present in the local voice component and deliberately unused; every task on the specification's list is still unticked.

One thing already diverges deliberately and says so. The specification asked for hold-to-speak; the implementation is click to start, click to stop. The argument that rejected automatic silence detection — that worldbuilding is full of long pauses, because people stop mid-sentence to decide whether the drowned god sings or sleeps — cuts against holding a key through those pauses just as hard.

What gets to come along

A shot with four characters and a location cites five reference pictures. The model you chose may accept four. Some accept one. Some accept none — all three of Arke's own local recipes accept none. Something has to give, and the decision cannot be silent: a quiet cap reads on screen as full coverage, and you end up blaming the model for drift that a rule you never saw actually caused.

So Arke ranks the candidates in a fixed order, takes what fits, and names what it dropped before you commit.

Characters go first because a face drifting is what a viewer notices; a room shifting by a shade is not. Within that, everyone cited gets their main picture before anybody gets a second — breadth before depth.

the sentence shown before you commit Seedance 2.0 accepts 4 references: carrying maren, alder, kess, harbour-mouth — dropping vessel-anchorage

Four things can run out, and Arke names which one bound the request, because they behave differently:

Ran out ofBehaviourWhy
Picture slots Drops by rank, names the drop The model accepts a fixed number and no more.
Distinct subjects Warns, never drops How many people a model holds apart describes where it starts degrading, not a wall. Dropping a character you wrote into the shot gives you a stable take of the wrong scene, with the warning that would have explained it replaced by the damage.

A cited character with no picture on file consumes no slot — their written description still travels in the words. And a model that accepts no pictures says so rather than silently sending none: "Local · Draft Image accepts no reference images — those images are omitted; only existing sheet descriptions remain for maren, alder."

Money

Every amount in Arke is a whole number of millionths of a dollar, with no decimal arithmetic anywhere and rounding done exactly once, at the moment a figure goes on screen. Prices reach fractions of a cent and a scene is dozens of them added up; a total assembled from decimals accumulates error you cannot see and cannot reconcile against a real invoice.

Knowing before you spend

Arke ships with a price list — the model manifest — covering every model it can dispatch to. It is a plain file inside the application, read at start-up and never fetched over the network: a product that works offline should not need the internet to tell you what a button costs.

Prices are not one shape. Video bills by the second and often differently per resolution, images per picture or per megapixel, speech per character, text per word in and per word out. The list carries each shape as its own kind rather than flattening them, so an estimate is arithmetic rather than a guess. A model absent from the list is refused, not attempted — dispatching without an estimate would break the one promise the app makes about money, which is that you see the figure before you commit.

The ledger

ledger.jsonl sits beside the job log at the application level. One line per finished job. Append-only, never rewritten, never compacted; the only repair it permits is trimming a final line left half-written by a crash, which removes nothing complete.

ledger.jsonl {"ts":"2026-07-30T14:02:04Z", "worldId":"01J8F3K2QW9VZX4N7M0RTYB6HC", "productionId":"saltlight", "jobId":"jb_01J8E0000000000000000000J2", "provider":"fal", "model":"seedance-2.0", "outcome":"succeeded", "estimatedMicroUsd":130000, "actualMicroUsd":128400, "actualSource":"provider-reported"}

Failures and cancellations get a line too. Arke cannot promise you were not billed for a job that failed, and cancelling after work has begun does not reliably prevent the charge. A ledger that omitted them would drift from your real invoice in exactly the direction that destroys trust in it.

actualSource is the field that keeps it honest. provider-reported means the service said what it charged; manifest-derived means nobody said anything, so the shipped estimate was recorded as the actual; local-zero means it ran on your own machine. A total mixing measured figures with derived ones is not a measured total, and Arke labels it as mixed.

The ledger keys on the world's permanent identifier — the long code in worldId — never on its folder name or its title. Rename a world and its whole spending history follows, because none of it was ever attached to a name. Renaming does not move the folder either: the folder is an address, the title is a word you should be free to rewrite.

Drift, and the rolling figure

A hand-written price list goes stale as providers change prices and retire models. Arke does not prevent that; it makes it visible. Because every line holds both the expectation and the charge, the gap is measurable: when one model's charges have diverged from its shipped price by more than fifteen per cent across at least three jobs, it is named on the spend screen with the size of the difference. Only genuinely reported charges count — comparing an estimate against an estimate would measure Arke's arithmetic against itself.

The figure Arke shows you is a rolling one: not a calendar month and not a running total since you installed it, but everything spent in the last N days counted backwards from now, seven by default. Tomorrow, today's spend is still in it and last Monday's has fallen out. It answers "what is this costing me lately" rather than "what have I ever spent", and it does not reset on the first of the month and flatter you.

It counts across all your worlds, because one key produces one bill however many worlds it was spent from. Cross a threshold you set and Arke tells you once, not on every job after. It alerts and never blocks: a hard stop mid-scene with a cut half assembled is worse than a warning, and the money is yours, on your own account.

Where your keys live

credentials.dat, at the application level beside the job log and the ledger — never inside a world. Each key is encrypted against a secret your operating system holds for your account, so the file is useless on another machine or under another login even if copied, and every write resets its permissions so only your account can read it.

Worlds are meant to be copied, zipped and handed to a collaborator. A key inside one would mean sharing your work shares your account, so sharing a world never shares a credential. The unencrypted key exists only in the memory of the running application, and is registered with the redaction boundary the moment you type it — anything that later tries to write it to a log finds it already scrubbed, rather than every logging call site being expected to remember.

The one that is not Arke's to hold

Higgsfield is different. Arke does not talk to it over the web at all; it drives Higgsfield's own command-line tool, which signs in for itself. That sign-in is a browser flow: Arke starts the tool, the tool opens your browser, you sign in there, and the tool keeps its own short-lived token. Arke waits — up to five minutes, then stops and says so, because a login left open forever is a stuck screen.

Nothing is copied out of that tool into credentials.dat: a second copy of a token the tool refreshes on its own schedule would be stale by design. So "is Higgsfield set up" is answered by asking the tool, not by looking for a file, and the answer can change while the app is running. If your sign-in reaches several billing accounts and none is picked, Arke says so before dispatching anything — a generation billed to the wrong account cannot be taken back.

The record of what was sent

provider-calls/calls.jsonl holds a redacted copy of every physical call Arke makes on your behalf: what went out, what came back, how long it took, which job it belonged to. It exists so that when something looks wrong you can see what was actually sent instead of reconstructing it from memory.

scrubbed
Any field whose name looks like a secret is replaced outright, every key Arke knows is scrubbed from the text, and the signed tail of every web address is cut off — those carry temporary access tokens.
yours only
Restricted to your account, like the credential file, and deliberately left out of the support bundle Arke can package for a bug report.
bounded
Past two thousand records or fifty megabytes it is compacted: the newest two thousand are rewritten and the rest dropped. A debugging aid that grows without limit is a disk problem you did not ask for.
both kinds
Web calls and command-line calls share one record, because they answer the same question. Neither is dressed as the other: an exit code is not a web status, and mapping one to the other would put a number in your history no service ever returned.

Generating on your own machine

Local generation exists so the iteration a shot goes through before it is worth paying for does not accrue per-second charges. Arke never exposes ComfyUI as such: what you get is a short menu of Arke-written recipes, sitting in the same picker as the paid models, chosen the same way, queued the same way, landing takes the same way, and written to the ledger the same way — at zero, labelled unmetered.

Local · Draft Image

Stills at 1024 across. Needs about 6 GB of graphics memory.

Local · Draft Video

Two, three or five seconds, wide or upright. Needs about 8 GB.

Local · Cloned Voice

Speech in a voice made from a recording that never leaves the machine. Needs about 8 GB.

A recipe is a fixed thing, not a workshop: no graph editor, no importing someone else's workflow. It is also a promise about what will happen and what it will need, so each names the exact model files and add-ons it depends on, down to a fingerprint of the file contents, checked immediately before every dispatch. A file that does not match what the recipe pinned switches the recipe off with that as the reason, rather than producing a picture from something else.

Speech and dictation run separately, through a small supervised helper Arke starts on your machine that listens only to itself: it reads text aloud in preset voices and transcribes push-to-talk dictation into any chat box. Cloud speech does not go through it — that takes the ordinary road, through the queue, with an errand number and a ledger line, so there is exactly one path where money is concerned.

Readiness, checked before you press anything

A text model degrades gracefully on a small machine. A video model that does not fit in graphics memory does not run at all. So Arke checks first, in order: is there an engine, does it answer, is it new enough, does it have the add-on this recipe needs, are the model files present, do they match their fingerprints, is the card big enough. The first failure switches the recipe off with that reason named — and the queue enforces the same answer on the way in, so nothing is written down for a dispatch the app already knows cannot run.

Two judgement calls are worth stating. A card big enough overall but mostly held by another program right now gets a different sentence from one that is simply too small — one will never work, the other is busy — and Arke assumes it can reclaim a few gigabytes by unloading the engine before it refuses. And if the card could not be measured at all, the recipe reads unknown and dispatches anyway: refusing on a machine that would have worked costs you the feature, while allowing one that then refuses in a quarter of a second costs you a click.

Missing is a state, not a bug

Arke assumes almost nothing about your machine. What is absent is reported with the reason, and the rest of the application carries on. Three real examples:

no encoder No video tooling

Arke uses a standard media encoder to draw the still that represents a clip, to measure whether a clip actually moves, and to export a finished cut. It ships inside the packaged application and is absent almost everywhere else. Without it, clips still generate, land, and can be watched and accepted — they simply have no thumbnail and no motion measurement, which is the state those screens have always handled. Only export refuses, and it says why. That split is deliberate: drawing a thumbnail happens while a paid take is being filed, and an error thrown there would destroy a generation you had already paid for.

no card Not enough graphics memory

A local recipe your machine cannot run is listed and switched off, with both figures and a way forward: "Needs 8 GB VRAM. This machine has 6 GB. Cloud video still works." Listed, not hidden — a menu that quietly omits things leaves you wondering what you are missing.

no key No credential stored

A job for a service with no key does not fail. Its lane pauses with the reason "no credential stored for this provider", the job returns to the front of the queue, and other services keep working. If Higgsfield's tool is not installed at all, every call returns "the Higgsfield CLI is not installed on this machine" — the remedy — rather than an obscure error that reads as though your shot failed to render.

The speech helper follows the same pattern. Not running, it names what is lost: "Voxa is not running — local voice and dictation are off; cloud voice still works."

What is written down when you spend money

Four things, in three places, none of which is a database. Arke keeps a search index to make screens fast, and that index is a cache: delete it and it rebuilds from the files below, every figure identical.

WrittenWhereWhat it holdsSurvives deleting the index
The job queue/jobs.jsonl Every state the job passed through, in order: what was asked for, what it was expected to cost, how many sends were authorised, how it ended. yes
The charge ledger.jsonl One line per finished job, failures and cancellations included, with the estimate and the actual kept apart and the source of the actual named. yes
The call provider-calls/calls.jsonl What was actually sent and what came back, secrets scrubbed, bounded in size, readable only by your account. yes
The take inside the world The media file itself and the record of what produced it. See takes & the cut. yes

All four survive, because in every case the log is the truth and the index is the convenience. The job log and the ledger sit outside every world by design: a world's index can be deleted, a world can be renamed, a world can be moved to another machine, and none of it touches what you spent or what you asked for.

The job log is append-only, so a busy month makes it long, not messy — and everything Arke shows you about your queue and your spending is worked out by reading those lines in order. If you ever want to check Arke's arithmetic, you can open the file.
