Generation and spend

Pressing the button

Everything Arke makes for you — a still, a six-second clip, a line of dialogue in a character's voice — travels down one road. The same road, whether the work runs on a service you pay by the second or on the graphics card in your own machine. This page is about that road, and about the paper trail it leaves.

The unit of work is a dispatch: one request, sent once, for one result. Arke calls the record of it a job. A job is not a button press; it is a durable row in a file that outlives the press, the window, and the machine being switched off mid-render.

What actually happens

  1. The request is assembled

    Not from the shot alone. Arke reads the shot's own action, the world's look, the scene's place and time of day, and the model sheets of whoever is in it. A character whose reference picture is going to travel with the request contributes the picture; one whose picture is not going contributes their written description instead, so nobody in the shot goes undescribed. The world's look is stated once at the top and restated once at the bottom as what must not drift — never repeated per beat, because a model given the same instruction four times spends its attention arguing with itself about which mention is authoritative.

  2. It is written down before it is sent

    A line is appended to queue/jobs.jsonl saying this job exists, what it is for, which model it goes to, and what Arke expects it to cost. Then a second line, saying Arke is about to send it. Only then does anything leave the machine. This order is the whole design and the next section is about why.

  3. It goes to the provider

    With an errand number attached — a one-off identifier Arke generated before the request and wrote down. Where a service honours such a number, it will recognise a repeat of the same errand and refuse to do the work twice. Jobs queue per provider, in the order you asked for them, at a rate Arke lowers by itself if the service says you are going too fast.

  4. The result is checked before it is kept

    The returned file is downloaded to a hidden staging folder, inspected to confirm it is really the kind of file it claims to be and that it arrived whole, and only then moved into your world under its final name. If a job returns several files and any one of them fails that check, none of them lands.

  5. The take and its charge are recorded

    The job's final state is appended to the job log, and exactly one charge line is appended to ledger.jsonl. Then the result becomes a take — what a take is, and how it reaches your cut, belongs to takes & the cut.

Written down before it happens, never after

There are two ways a program that spends your money can betray you, and they are opposites. It can lose work you paid for. Or it can pay twice for work you asked for once. A queue that only records what it intends to do prevents the first and causes the second, because after a crash an unsent request and a sent-but-unwitnessed request look exactly the same — and the obvious recovery, sending it, is the double charge.

So Arke's rule is: every change of state is written down before the action it authorises. Not after. The instant before the request leaves the machine, the job log already says a request is leaving. If the power goes at that instant, what remains on disk is a record of something in flight — not a gap.

Never trust silence

The second half of the rule matters more. When Arke restarts and finds a job that reads submitting, it does not know what happened. It does not guess. A gap in what Arke could observe is treated as unknown, to be found out — never as a failure to quietly retry.

Why that is worth the trouble: a retry is not free. Guessing "it probably never sent" charges you twice for one clip. Guessing "it probably did" throws away work you have already paid for. Neither guess is cheap, and no amount of cleverness makes one of them safe, so Arke goes and looks instead.

How it looks depends on what the service can be asked:

Ask by number
Where the service can be asked "did you receive errand 01J8E1?", the answer is definite both ways. Found means the work exists — Arke adopts it and starts watching it. Not found means it never arrived — Arke sends it, safely.
Look at the recent list
Where the service can only list its recent work, a match is conclusive. Absence is only conclusive if the list reaches back further than the moment in question — Arke checks that the oldest entry in the list predates the submission before it believes a negative.
Ask you
Where the service can do neither, the job is parked and you are asked, with the cost of a possible duplicate stated in plain money: "Arke did not witness the submission result. fal may have accepted and charged it, and cannot confirm what happened. No automatic retry was made. Resubmitting may charge about $0.13 again; the prior actual cost is unknown." This is not a hole in the design. It is the only honest position when nobody can say what happened.

Reconciliation runs at start-up and finishes before the queue will accept any new work. Coming back to forty jobs you left running, you are told what Arke found rather than being handed a queue that quietly resumed on assumptions.

One deliberate exception to the ask-first rule: a job that already has the service's own reference number for the work resumes by watching, never by resending. There is nothing unknown about it.

What a job line actually says

queue/jobs.jsonl lives at the application level, not inside a world, because jobs from every world you have open compete for one account's rate limit and one machine's graphics card. It is append-only: a job is never edited in place, it gains a new complete line each time it changes. Reading the file top to bottom and keeping the last line for each job gives you the present; reading it in order gives you the history.

Here is a real line from the sample world, wrapped to fit:

queue\jobs.jsonl {"id":"jb_01J8E0000000000000000000J2", "idempotencyKey":"01J8E1000000000000000000K2", "worldId":"01J8F3K2QW9VZX4N7M0RTYB6HC", "productionId":"saltlight", "target":{"kind":"shot","id":"sh_12","coversShots":["sh_12"]}, "provider":"fal", "model":"seedance-2.0", "capability":"video", "params":{"aspect":"16:9","durationSec":6}, "estimatedMicroUsd":130000, "status":"succeeded", "providerJobId":"fal_q3c", "attempt":1, "error":null, "createdAt":"2026-07-30T14:01:05Z", "updatedAt":"2026-07-30T14:02:04Z"}

Nothing in that line is a secret, a file path outside the world, or a piece of prose. It is safe to read, safe to send someone, and readable a decade from now with any text editor.

The parts worth knowing

status
One of seven words: queued, submitting, running, succeeded, failed, cancelled, and needs-reconciliation — the one that means Arke could not observe what happened and is waiting on you.
target
What the job is for, in the caller's own words: a shot, a whole scene pass, a model sheet, a voice line, a reference tile. The queue itself does not know what any of those mean, which is exactly why one queue can serve all of them.
provider, model
Which service, and which specific model on it. Never just "a video provider" — a default that resolved only to a company would give you a different price and different capabilities on different days.
estimatedMicroUsd
What Arke expected this to cost, in millionths of a dollar, worked out before the request left. 130000 is thirteen cents.
attempt
How many physical sends have been authorised for this job — written down before each one. A crash may make Arke overcount a send by one; it can never undercount, which is the direction that matters when a send costs money.
idempotencyKey
The errand number. Minted and written down before the first send, and reused for the life of the job.
deletedAt
Removing a finished job from your Activity list appends a final line marking it gone, rather than cutting a line out of the file. Its charge line in the ledger is untouched: what was spent stays spent.

A reconcile action

When Arke starts, it works through every unfinished job and produces a short report of what it did to each. That entry is a reconcile action, and there are seven possible answers: adopted (the work was found at the provider and is now being watched), resubmitted (it was provably never received), resumed polling (it had a reference number all along), requeued, failed, ledger-completed (the job had finished but its charge line was missing, so it was written), and held for user — the honest one, where you are asked.

When something goes wrong

Retrying the wrong thing is expensive in a way that retrying an ordinary web page is not. A content refusal retried five times is five charges for the same refusal. So failures are sorted into four kinds and each kind gets different behaviour.

KindLooks likeWhat Arke does
Too fast, or a hiccup Rate limit, a server error, a timeout, a dropped connection Waits and tries again, up to four sends. Each wait is roughly double the last with a random fraction shaved off, capped at thirty seconds. A rate limit additionally tells Arke to slow that service's whole lane down.
Refused Content policy, invalid settings, a model that does not exist No retry at all. The reason is shown. Sending it again would buy the same refusal.
Your account A rejected key, a revoked key, quota gone, billing failed The whole lane for that service pauses and you are told once. Queued jobs stay queued rather than failing one by one — they were never wrong, the credential was. Other services keep running: a bad FAL key does not stop ElevenLabs.
Offline No network at all Jobs stay queued. Nothing about them is wrong. Closing a laptop mid-scene and reopening it elsewhere should give you a queue that carries on, not a screen of errors.

An error Arke cannot recognise is treated as refused, not as a hiccup. Failing visibly is cheaper than retrying wrongly.

Checking the file before keeping it

Every returned file is inspected at the level a download failure actually breaks. Arke reads the first few bytes to confirm the file is what it claims — a picture really starts like a picture, a clip really carries the box that marks it as one — and then reads the end of the file to confirm the closing marker is there. An empty download is refused outright.

For pictures it goes one step further: whatever the service said it was sending, Arke reads the bytes to decide whether it is really a PNG, a JPEG or a WebP, and names the landed file accordingly. A file that fails any of this is not kept, and the job is failed with the reason.

Why an unverified file is worse than a failure: a clip that was cut off in transit still plays. It plays for two seconds and stops. You watch it, decide the model got the shot wrong, and generate again — paying a second time to fix a problem that was never the model's. A failed job tells you the truth immediately and costs you one look at an error message. A half-downloaded file lies to you and costs you another generation.

What gets to come along

A shot with four characters and a location in it cites five reference pictures. The model you have chosen may accept four. Some accept one. Some accept none at all — all three of Arke's own local recipes accept none. Something has to give, and the decision cannot be silent: a quiet cap reads on screen as full coverage, and you end up blaming the model for drift that a rule you never saw actually caused.

So Arke ranks the candidates by a fixed order, takes what fits, and names what it dropped before you commit.

Characters go first because a face drifting is the thing a viewer notices; a room that shifts by a shade is not. Within that, everyone cited gets their main picture before anybody gets a second one — breadth before depth. A character with both a model sheet and a photograph can spend a spare slot on the second image, but only once every other character has their first.

The notice you see is specific about what was lost:

the sentence shown before you commit Seedance 2.0 accepts 4 references: carrying maren, alder, kess, harbour-mouth — dropping vessel-anchorage

Four separate things can run out, and Arke names which one bound the request, because they behave differently and you need to know which one to act on.

Ran out ofBehaviourWhy
Picture slots Drops by rank, names the drop The model accepts a fixed number and no more.
Distinct subjects Warns. Never drops. The number of people a model can hold apart describes where it starts degrading, not a wall. Dropping a character you wrote into the shot produces a stable take of the wrong scene — with the warning that would have explained it replaced by the damage.
Seconds of sound or motion Refuses the one that does not fit Where a model takes an audio or video reference at all, it is budgeted in seconds, because seconds are what can actually be proved. A clip whose length could not be measured is refused rather than assumed to be zero — zero is precisely what would make it fit.
Size on the wire Blocks Unlike the others this does not degrade. Past the ceiling the request is one the service is already known to reject, and naming a certain failure while still letting you press the button is the warning-versus-refusal distinction applied backwards.

A cited character with no picture on file never consumes a slot at all — their written description still travels in the words. And a model that accepts no pictures says so rather than silently sending none: "Local · Draft Image accepts no reference images — those images are omitted; only existing sheet descriptions remain for maren, alder."

Money

Every amount in Arke is a whole number of millionths of a dollar. There is deliberately no decimal arithmetic anywhere: prices reach fractions of a cent, a scene is dozens of them added up, and rounding is done exactly once, at the moment a figure is put on screen. A total assembled from decimals accumulates error you cannot see and cannot reconcile against a real invoice.

Knowing before you spend

Arke ships with a price list — the model manifest — covering every model it can dispatch to. It is a plain file inside the application, read at start-up and never fetched over the network, because a product that works offline should not need the internet to tell you what a button costs, and a price list downloaded at run time is one more thing that can be tampered with.

Prices are not one shape. Video bills by the second and often differently per resolution; images bill per picture or per megapixel; speech bills per character; text bills per word in and per word out. The list carries each shape as its own kind rather than flattening them, so the estimate is arithmetic rather than a guess. A model that is not in the list is refused, not attempted — dispatching without an estimate would break the one promise the app makes about money, which is that you see the figure before you commit.

The ledger

ledger.jsonl sits beside the job log at the application level. One line per finished job. Append-only, never rewritten, never compacted; the only repair it permits is trimming a final line left half-written by a crash, which removes nothing complete.

ledger.jsonl {"ts":"2026-07-30T14:02:04Z", "worldId":"01J8F3K2QW9VZX4N7M0RTYB6HC", "productionId":"saltlight", "jobId":"jb_01J8E0000000000000000000J2", "provider":"fal", "model":"seedance-2.0", "outcome":"succeeded", "estimatedMicroUsd":130000, "actualMicroUsd":128400, "actualSource":"provider-reported"}

Failures and cancellations get a line too. Arke cannot promise you were not billed for a job that failed, so it records what the service said rather than asserting a zero. Cancelling after work has started does not reliably prevent the charge either, and a ledger that omitted cancellations would drift away from your real invoice in exactly the direction that destroys trust in it.

actualSource is the field that keeps the whole thing honest. It says where the number came from: provider-reported means the service told Arke what it charged, manifest-derived means nobody told Arke anything so the shipped estimate was recorded as the actual, and local-zero means it ran on your own machine and cost nothing to meter. A total that mixes measured figures with derived ones is not a measured total, and Arke labels it as mixed rather than presenting one number as though it were all the same kind.

The ledger keys on the world's permanent identifier — the long code in worldId — and never on its folder name or its title. Rename a world and its entire spending history follows it, because nothing about that history was ever attached to a name. Renaming a world in Arke does not move its folder for the same reason: the folder is an address, and the title is a word you should be free to rewrite.

Drift

A hand-written price list goes stale. Providers change prices and retire models. Arke does not try to prevent that; it makes it visible. Because every line holds both what was expected and what was actually charged, the gap between the two is measurable. When one model's charges have diverged from its shipped price by more than fifteen per cent, and it has done so across at least three jobs, that model is named on the spend screen along with the size of the difference.

Only genuinely reported charges count towards this. Comparing an estimate against an estimate would only measure Arke's own arithmetic against itself.

The rolling figure and the alert

The number Arke shows you is a rolling one: not a calendar month and not a running total since you installed it, but everything spent in the last N days, counted backwards from right now. Seven days by default. Tomorrow, today's spend is still in it and last Monday's has fallen out. It answers "what is this costing me lately" rather than "what have I ever spent", and it does not reset on the first of the month and flatter you.

It counts across all your worlds, because one key produces one bill regardless of how many worlds it was spent from. Set a threshold and Arke tells you when the rolling figure crosses it — once, not on every job after. It alerts and never blocks. A hard stop mid-scene with a cut half assembled is worse than a warning, and the money is yours, on your own account.

Where your keys live

credentials.dat, at the application level, next to the job log and the ledger — never inside a world. Each key is encrypted against a secret your operating system holds for your account, so the file is useless on another machine or under another login even if it is copied. Every time it is written, its permissions are reset so that only your account can read it, with anything inherited from the folder above stripped away.

The reason it lives outside every world is simple: worlds are meant to be copied, zipped, handed to a collaborator, and moved between machines. If a key were inside one, sharing your work would share your account. So sharing a world never shares a credential. The unencrypted key exists only in the memory of the running application, and is registered with the redaction boundary the moment you type it — so anything that later tries to write it to a log finds it already scrubbed, rather than every logging call site being expected to remember.

The one that is not Arke's to hold

Higgsfield is different. Arke does not talk to it over the web at all; it drives Higgsfield's own command-line tool, which signs in for itself. That sign-in is a browser flow: Arke starts the tool, the tool opens your browser, you sign in there, and the tool keeps its own short-lived token. Arke waits — for up to five minutes, after which it stops waiting and says so, because a login left open forever is a stuck screen.

Nothing is copied out of that tool and into credentials.dat. A second copy of a token the tool refreshes on its own schedule would be stale by design. So for Higgsfield, "is it set up" is a question Arke answers by asking the tool, not by looking for a file, and the answer can change while the app is running. If your sign-in reaches several billing accounts and none has been picked, Arke says so before dispatching anything: a generation billed to the wrong account cannot be taken back.

The record of what was sent

provider-calls/calls.jsonl holds a redacted copy of every physical call Arke makes on your behalf — what went out, what came back, how long it took, and which job it belonged to. It exists so that when something looks wrong you can see what was actually sent rather than reconstructing it from memory.

scrubbed
Any field whose name looks like a secret is replaced outright, every key Arke knows about is scrubbed from the text, and the signed part of every web address is cut off — those carry temporary access tokens.
yours only
The file's permissions are restricted to your account, the same way the credential file is. It is deliberately left out of the support bundle Arke can package up for a bug report.
bounded
Past two thousand records or fifty megabytes it is compacted — the newest two thousand are rewritten and the rest dropped. A debugging aid that grows without limit is a disk problem you did not ask for.
both kinds
Web calls and command-line calls share the record, because they answer the same question. Neither is dressed up as the other: an exit code is not a web status, and mapping one to the other would put a number in your history that no service ever returned.

Generating on your own machine

Local generation exists so that the iteration a shot goes through before it is worth paying for does not accrue per-second charges. Arke never exposes ComfyUI as such. What you get is a short menu of Arke-written recipes, sitting in the same picker as the paid models, chosen the same way, queued the same way, landing takes the same way, and written to the ledger the same way — at zero, labelled unmetered.

Local · Draft Image

Stills at 1024 across. Needs about 6 GB of graphics memory.

Local · Draft Video

Two, three or five seconds, upright or wide. Needs about 8 GB.

Local · Cloned Voice

Speech in a voice made from a recording that never leaves the machine. Needs about 8 GB.

A recipe is a fixed thing, not a workshop. There is no graph editor and no importing somebody else's workflow, because a recipe is also a promise about what will happen and what it will need. Each one names the exact model files and add-ons it depends on, down to a fingerprint of the file contents, and those are checked immediately before every dispatch — a file that does not match what the recipe pinned turns the recipe off with that as the reason, rather than producing a picture from something else.

Separately, speech and dictation run through a small supervised helper Arke starts on your machine, listening only to itself. It reads text aloud in preset voices and transcribes push-to-talk dictation into any chat box. Cloud speech does not go through it — that travels the ordinary road, through the queue, with an errand number and a ledger line, so there is exactly one path where money is concerned.

Readiness, checked before you press anything

A language model degrades gracefully when your machine is small. A video model that does not fit in graphics memory does not run at all. So Arke checks first, in order: is there an engine, does it answer, is it new enough, does it have the add-on this recipe needs, are the model files present, do they match their fingerprints, and is the graphics card big enough. The first thing that fails switches the recipe off with that reason named — and enqueue enforces the same answer, so nothing is written down for a dispatch the app already knows cannot run.

Two refinements worth stating, because both are judgement calls. If your card is big enough overall but another program is holding most of it right now, that gets a different sentence from a card that is simply too small — one will never work, the other is busy — and Arke assumes it can reclaim a few gigabytes by unloading the engine before it refuses. And if the card could not be measured at all, the recipe reads unknown and dispatches anyway, because refusing on a machine that would have worked costs you the feature, while allowing one that then refuses in a quarter of a second costs you a click.

Missing is a state, not a bug

Arke assumes almost nothing about your machine. What is absent is reported with the reason, and the rest of the application carries on. Three examples, all real:

no encoder No video tooling

Arke uses a standard media encoder for three things: drawing the still that represents a clip, measuring whether a clip actually moves, and exporting a finished cut. It ships inside the packaged application and is absent almost everywhere else. Without it, clips still generate, land, and can be watched and accepted — they just have no thumbnail and no motion measurement, which is the state those screens have always handled. Only export refuses, and it says why. This is deliberate: drawing a thumbnail happens while a paid take is being filed, and an error thrown there would destroy a generation you had already paid for.

no card Not enough graphics memory

A local recipe your machine cannot run is listed and switched off, with both figures and a way forward: "Needs 8 GB VRAM. This machine has 6 GB. Cloud video still works." It is not hidden, because a menu that quietly omits things leaves you wondering what you are missing.

no key No credential stored

A job for a service with no key does not fail. Its lane pauses with the reason "no credential stored for this provider", the job goes back to the front of the queue, and other services keep working. The job was never wrong. And if Higgsfield's tool is not installed at all, every call to it returns "the Higgsfield CLI is not installed on this machine" — the remedy — rather than an obscure error that reads as though your shot failed to render.

The speech helper follows the same pattern. Not running, it says so and names what is lost: "Voxa is not running — local voice and dictation are off; cloud voice still works."

What is written down when you spend money

Four things, in three places, none of which is a database. Arke keeps a search index to make screens fast — counts, lists, what cites what — and that index is a cache. Delete it and it rebuilds itself from the files below, with every figure identical. It is structurally prevented from being the only place anything lives.

WrittenWhereWhat it holdsSurvives deleting the index
The job queue/jobs.jsonl Every state the job passed through, in order — what was asked for, what it was expected to cost, how many sends were authorised, and how it ended. yes
The charge ledger.jsonl One line per finished job, including failures and cancellations, with the estimate and the actual kept apart and the source of the actual named. yes
The call provider-calls/calls.jsonl What was actually sent and what came back, with secrets scrubbed. Kept to a bounded size and readable only by your account. yes
The take inside the world The media file itself and the record of what produced it. See takes & the cut. yes

All four survive, because in every case the log is the truth and the index is the convenience. The job log and the ledger sit outside every world by design: a world's index can be deleted, a world can be renamed, a world can be moved to another machine, and none of it touches what you spent or what you asked for.

One consequence worth knowing. The job log is append-only, so a busy month makes it long, not messy. Everything Arke shows you about your queue and your spending is worked out by reading those lines in order. If you ever want to check Arke's arithmetic, you can open the file.
