# AI tells: the six patterns, and why density is the tell

**Gathered:** 2026-10-19. **Informs:** [design turn 232](../../design-system/prose-review-232.notes.md),
the chapter review's AI tells check (#1744, #1782).

What a reader, an agent or an editor notices in prose an AI model wrote or rewrote, written down
precisely enough for a reviewer to count it. This note is the definition the check is built from.
The decisions about it (the line, what the screen shows, what the writer can do) are turn 232's.

## The source, and how strong it is

One practitioner's account: an episode of *The Novelist Studio*, a YouTube channel about using AI
as a craft writer without outsourcing the writing. The owner supplied a transcript on
2026-10-19; the episode's URL was not recorded and a search did not find it. Add it here when known.

The host reads two AI-written 450-word openings aloud and marks each tell as it occurs:

- a thriller opening attributed to ChatGPT
- a romance opening attributed to Claude

The host gives a count for each sample. The account's central claim is the one this design rests on:

> These devices are ordinary craft. Writers use the rule of three and metaphor on purpose, and
> doing so will not get a manuscript flagged. What gives AI-written prose away is that the devices
> occur densely, almost every paragraph, and in the same way across many manuscripts.

The account also connects this to a practical stake. The host reports that U.S. Copyright Office
correspondence has asked applicants to identify which sentences are their own when a submission
shows these patterns. That is the host's report, not something this note has checked.

**Evidence strength, plainly.**

- This is one experienced reader's judgement over two samples of about 450 words each.
- The counts are that reader's tagging. Nobody checked them independently, and some definitions
  overlap.
- There is **no human baseline**: the account does not count a human-written passage. So it says
  how dense AI prose was, not where human prose sits.
- It is good evidence for *which* patterns to look for and *that* density, rather than presence,
  is the signal. It is not evidence for any particular threshold.

## The counts the source gave

| Sample (about 451 words) | Rule of three | Stacked list | Metaphor | Not X but Y | Pivot sentence | Emotional shorthand | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Thriller, attributed to ChatGPT | 2 | 4 | 8 | 2 | not counted | not counted | about 16 |
| Romance, attributed to Claude | 3 | 3 | 5 | 2 | 5 | 3 | 21 |

That is one tell every 21 to 28 words, roughly 35 to 47 per 1,000 words. The thriller total is
approximate: the host's running count and summary differ by one in places, and that sample was
not checked for pivots or shorthand.

The account also names emotional flatlining and constant emotional drama ("almost every
paragraph") as things agents spot. It does not define or count either one, so neither is a tell
here.

## The six tells

Each entry gives:

- the label on screen
- what counts as one instance
- what does not count
- examples, from the source (paraphrased or trimmed) and from turn 232's drawing

The **Not** lines are this note's reading, not the source's. They are added so a reviewer can
apply the kinds the same way twice.

The reviewer's brief should carry the definitions. It should not carry the examples as a
pattern list, because a model told to find "Past the dancers" will find exactly that.

### Rule of three

**One instance:** three parallel beats, as words, phrases or whole sentences. They usually build,
or repeat a frame. The rhythm does the work, not the information.

- Source: *Mara was unstable. Mara was paranoid. Mara needed help.*
- Source: *generously, loudly, somewhat carelessly*
- Source: *shame, then tenderness, then a dangerous aching protectiveness*
- Source: three sentences opening *He could…*
- Drawing: *Past the dancers. Past the bar. Past the last of his good sense.*

**Not:** three items that are simply the facts, such as who is in the room or what was bought.
Not four or more concrete items either; that is a stacked list.

### Stacked list

The source calls this "list rhythm stacking."

**One instance:** a run of three or more concrete details, often sentence fragments of the same
shape. They are piled up for atmosphere or emotional weight, and often end on a twist item.

- Source: the seven-minute gap, the click of the camera, the nurse's shoes on one cracked tile
- Source: *weddings, funerals, layoffs, terrible haircuts, and one disastrous investment…*
- Source: the glances, the private jokes, the way she would wait for a truer answer
- Drawing: *The champagne carried in under sparklers. The men who laughed with their wallets. …*

**Not:** a list that carries information the scene needs, said once. If a run is exactly three
abstract beats, it is a rule of three. If it is concrete things catalogued, it is a stacked list.
Count it once, as whichever fits better.

### Metaphor

This includes personification. The source calls it "metaphor stacking."

**One instance:** one figure. That is a simile (*like*, *as if*, *as though*), a metaphor, or an
object, room or feeling given an action or a will.

- Source: *as though kindness could be used as a gag*
- Source: *listening to the building breathe*
- Source: *the heater coughed on*
- Source: *fluent in restraint*
- Source: *the shape of the cage*
- Drawing: *held the whole loud room at arm's length and made it wait*

**Not:** dead figures in everyday speech, such as *fell for her* or *a cold look*, or a figure a
character says aloud as characterisation. Four or more in one stretch is "stacking" in itself;
see the kind line in turn 232.

### Not X but Y

**One instance:** defining something by first denying an alternative. Its forms:

- *It wasn't X, it was Y.*
- *Not X, not Y, but Z.*
- *It was not only X… It was Y.*
- *Not a key, not exactly, but…*
- The subtler version the source names: *She did not X, which would have been easier…; she did Y.*

Examples:

- Source: *Not dramatically, not with the tidy violence of lust, but with the dull, irreversible click of a lock turning*
- Drawing: *It was not her beauty that stopped him halfway. It was the stillness*

**Not:** a plain negative that does not set up a reveal, such as *She did not answer.*

### Pivot sentence

The source calls these "clean pivot sentences."

**One instance:** a short, clean declarative, often its own paragraph or the first or last line of
one. Its job is to turn the scene or land a beat, not to move the action.

- Source: *Julian looked away too late.*
- Source: *So Julian became disciplined.*
- Source: *He had known Elise for eight months.*
- Drawing: *He stopped.* and *She looked up.*

**Not:** short sentences that are fast action in a sequence, or ordinary short dialogue. This is
the most judgement-dependent kind. The source counted five in 451 words, the same as its
metaphors.

### Emotional shorthand

**One instance:** a feeling named or summed up in an abstract, quotable line, often an aphorism
about hearts, truths or wanting, instead of being shown through what someone does.

- Source: *the heart could be loyal and treacherous in the same beat*
- Source: *she sometimes felt lonely in a room full of people*
- Source: *he understood with a clarity that frightened him*
- Drawing: *Something in his chest had shifted, quietly and for good*

**Not:** a character stating a feeling in dialogue, or a plain report of one (*She was angry.*).

### Overlaps

One sentence can carry two kinds, and both count. The source counted *not with the tidy violence
of lust, but with the dull, irreversible click of a lock turning* as Not X but Y and as Metaphor.

Two tags of the same kind that begin at the same place are one instance. A tag that begins
inside another of its kind is a separate one: the first may run on past its own device.

## What this means for a check

The source's claim turns into three properties the check has to have. Turn 232 draws them.

1. **Count, then judge density; never flag an instance.** One tell is craft. The finding is a
   stretch of prose where tells crowd together.
2. **Use a fixed stretch.** The source's samples are about 450 words, a page or so, which is also
   about what a reader takes in before forming an impression. Counting per 450 words makes a
   passage comparable with the source's numbers.
3. **Find a human baseline before trusting a line.** The source gives two AI points and no human
   one. Any threshold drawn from it alone is a guess. The calibration plan is in turn 232's notes.
