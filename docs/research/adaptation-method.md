# From research to the agent: the adaptation method

**Written:** 2026-10-08. **Draws on:** [Adapting a novel for the screen](novel-to-screen-adaptation.md),
[The vertical microdrama format](microdrama-format.md),
[Short drama for African audiences](african-short-drama.md).

What Arke's agent does when a production is adapted from a story already in the world — first a
microdrama, the same method for a film or a series. The author decides; the agent proposes, with
reasons, and never cuts silently. This note is the method in plain terms; the binding version is
the design turn and the spec that cite it.

## 1. The target, and its numbers

The author picks what the story becomes and the numbers it is held to. Nothing here is fixed: each
number is a starting value the author can change, and the agent says what a change comes to.

The first question is **where it will be watched**. The global vertical norms do not transfer to
African audiences: the paywall apps have Nigerian viewers and almost no Nigerian revenue, and the
African-made form that broke out was free ([african-short-drama.md](african-short-drama.md)). The
agent proposes a preset from the story's setting and says why.

| Preset | Episodes | Episode length | Release | Free |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Micro drama · Nigeria, free vertical | 40–60 | 60–90 s | Daily | All |
| Micro drama · global app | 60–100 | 60–120 s | All at once | First 8, then paid |
| Micro drama · East Africa | 30–60 | 2–3 min | Daily | All |
| Micro drama · South Africa | About 60 | 1–3 min | Daily | First 8, then tokens |
| Micro drama · francophone | 5–12 | 3–6 min | Weekly | All |
| Film · YouTube Nollywood | 1, or 3–8 parts | 90–120 min, or parts of 15–40 min | Weekly | All |
| Limited series | 6–12 | 20–50 min | Weekly | — |
| Film | 1 | 90–120 min | — | — |

About 90 seconds is the default episode for a vertical micro drama and moves with the preset.
Every micro drama keeps a hook in its first seconds and an ending on a question; where nothing is
paid for, each ending has to bring the viewer back tomorrow rather than to a paywall.

A production can hold a season of at most 50 episodes today, so a longer adaptation is planned as
seasons.

## 2. The spine, and what must survive

Agreed with the author before anything is cut:

- **The central question** the story asks, and the answer its ending gives.
- **The protagonist's want and need,** who opposes them and why, and the theme.
- **The promise** of this version: genre, the central fantasy, what each episode keeps delivering.
- **Must survive:** the tone, the scenes and lines the author would grieve to lose, the images that
  carry the book, and the author's content limits.

## 3. The inventory

The book broken into dramatic units, not chapters. Where a chapter was grouped by beats for its
audiobook (design turn 201), Arke already holds its units: each named beat, with its words, whose
beat it is and its length. Where it was not, the agent reads the chapter and names its units the
same way, and keeps them with the adaptation. Beside them the agent lists:

- **Characters,** each with the job they do in the story.
- **Subplots,** each with its setup, turns and payoff.
- **Peaks:** the moments a viewer would come back for — reversals, reveals, public scenes, the
  milestones of the romance. A long novel has perhaps thirty to forty.

## 4. The verdicts

Every unit, character and subplot gets one verdict and a reason tied to the spine:

| Verdict | When |
| --- | --- |
| **Keep** | It serves the spine and can be shown as it is |
| **Cut** | It serves neither the central question, the theme, nor a motivation |
| **Merge** | Something else already does its job — two characters into one, three scenes into one |
| **Show** | It is inner life: turn it into behaviour, an object, staging, dialogue with subtext, or a single line of voiceover, in that order |
| **Expand** | The book summarises what should be a scene, or implies a relationship the screen must give time |
| **Invent** | The screen needs a scene the book lacks; it must trace to the book's evidence or the author's intent |

The questions behind a verdict: does it serve the spine; does something else do its job; has it its
own setup and payoff; can it be shown; does it happen because of the protagonist; is it summarised
where it should be a scene; would the author grieve its loss; is the audience already seeing it.

The author can overrule any verdict. Nothing is cut silently; a cut stays listed, so it can come back.

## 5. The season

For a microdrama, the peaks are mapped to episodes — never the chapters in order:

- **Every episode:** a hook in the first seconds a stranger could follow, one turn, and an ending on
  a question.
- **The first ten** carry the most peaks and the most care.
- **The paywall episode** (where there is one) lands just after a small payoff; the big reveals come
  one or two episodes later.
- **An escalation** about every ten episodes.
- **A slow burn** becomes a ladder of milestones, the leads put in each other's way in episode one.

## 6. The episodes, and the checks

Each episode becomes scenes, two or three locations and two or three people a scene, dialogue
compressed and said plainly, each scene citing the chapters and beats it came from. Then:

- **An agency pass:** does the protagonist cause what happens?
- **A weight pass on the beloved:** a novel told close on one lover leaves the other needing scenes
  of her own, or her choices read as cruelty.
- **Production limits** and **the author's content limits.** The format pulls toward public
  humiliation and power-imbalance tropes; the agent names that pull and follows the author's limits.
  For a Nigerian release of a story with juju or ritual, it proposes one limit — never glamorised —
  and reminds the author once that NFVCB classification applies, YouTube included.
- **Season checks:** repeated hooks, absent characters, unresolved promises, stalled arcs.

## 7. After it is made

The adaptation stays linked to its source. Each episode and scene cites its chapters and beats; when
the novel changes, the scenes that came from the changed part say so. Nothing is rewritten on its own.
