# Adapting a novel for the screen

**Gathered:** 2026-10-08, by web research. **Used by:** the adaptation method
([adaptation-method.md](adaptation-method.md)).

How screenwriters turn a novel into a film or a series: the process they follow, what they cut,
what they change, what they add, and how they decide. Linda Seger's *The Art of Adaptation* and
Kenneth Portnoy's *Screen Adaptation* could be checked only at table-of-contents level; everything
else below was read at its source unless marked otherwise.

## The process

1. **Read for what the story is about before planning anything.** Write the story in a sentence and
   treat that as the main line; define the protagonist's want, need and goal, and who opposes them
   and why ([Kouguell, Script Magazine](https://scriptmag.com/columns/adapting-novels-memoirs-short-stories-keep-cut)).
   Bowerman works in layers: theme and outer motivation first, cut what serves neither, then add
   the inner motivation and cut what serves none of the three
   ([Writer's Digest](https://www.writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/take-two-how-to-adapt-a-book-into-a-screenplay)).
2. **Ask why this book resists the screen.** Seger's book opens on why literature resists film, and
   orders the work as story, characters, theme, then style, mood and tone
   ([Internet Archive record](https://archive.org/details/artofadaptationt0000sege)). McKee's first
   principle of adaptation warns that the more a novel lives inside one character's inner conflict,
   the harder it is to film
   ([reader highlight of *Story*](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/40389794-story/27114303-chandler/5a040187-c684-43e9-92f0-165b31a5a43f)).
3. **Break the novel into dramatic units, not chapters.** Coppola cut *The Godfather* into a binder:
   five acts and about fifty scenes, ignoring the chapter breaks, each scene with a sheet for its
   synopsis, the times, imagery and tone, its core, and its pitfalls
   ([Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/the-godfather-notebook/)).
4. **Beat-sheet the events, then choose the through-line,** without feeling bound to the novel's
   order ([Kouguell](https://scriptmag.com/columns/adapting-novels-memoirs-short-stories-keep-cut);
   [Bowerman](https://www.writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/take-two-how-to-adapt-a-book-into-a-screenplay)).
5. **Build the screen structure, and change the time scheme if it serves.** Gerwig opened *Little
   Women* on the adult Jo and cut between two timelines
   ([Scriptnotes 433](https://johnaugust.com/2020/scriptnotes-ep-433-the-one-with-greta-gerwig));
   Hampton intercut what *Atonement* tells in blocks
   ([PopEntertainment](https://popentertainmentarchives.com/post/christopher-hampton-adapting-atonement)).
6. **Step away from the book.** Flynn reportedly read *Gone Girl* once more as an audiobook and then
   went back to it only for dialogue, with a note above her desk reminding her it was a movie
   ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2014/11/hollywood-reporter-screenwriter-roundtable)).
7. **Cut, then cut again** ([Kouguell](https://scriptmag.com/columns/adapting-novels-memoirs-short-stories-keep-cut)).

**Length, as rules of thumb.** A script page is about a minute, so a feature is about 110 pages and
a 400-page novel cannot fit in two hours
([Bowerman](https://www.writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/take-two-how-to-adapt-a-book-into-a-screenplay));
*The Silence of the Lambs* went from about 350 pages to a 120-page script
([Field, *Four Screenplays*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/49324/four-screenplays-by-syd-field/)).
A limited series changes the arithmetic: *Normal People* ran twelve episodes so viewers could spend
as long with the couple as readers do
([New Statesman](https://www.newstatesman.com/long-reads/2020/04/lenny-abrahamson-normal-people-tv-show-bbc-three-adaptation)).

## What is cut

| Cut | Why | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subplots that serve neither theme nor motivation | They dilute the spine; *The Godfather* reduced Lucy Mancini's Las Vegas arc to brief scenes | [Washington Post via elenaferrante.com](https://elenaferrante.com/tv-series/the-washington-post-what-the-women-of-my-brilliant-friend-tell-us-about-the-men-of-the-godfather/) |
| Characters doing the same job | Note each character's purpose; cut or merge into composites | [Kouguell](https://scriptmag.com/columns/adapting-novels-memoirs-short-stories-keep-cut) |
| Inner thought, long description, most flashbacks | They slow the pace unless they are a structural choice | [Kouguell](https://scriptmag.com/columns/adapting-novels-memoirs-short-stories-keep-cut) |
| Framing devices that explain | Wright removed *Atonement*'s old-Briony frame so the ending surprises | [PopEntertainment](https://popentertainmentarchives.com/post/christopher-hampton-adapting-atonement) |
| Set pieces the budget cannot carry | *Atonement*'s Dunkirk became one long take | [PopEntertainment](https://popentertainmentarchives.com/post/christopher-hampton-adapting-atonement) |
| What makes a lead less sympathetic than the story needs | Thompson replaced Edward's ring and letter with an embroidered handkerchief | [Stovel, JASNA](https://www.jasna.org/persuasions/on-line/vol32no1/stovel.html) |

## What is changed

- **Inner life becomes behaviour, an object, dialogue with subtext, or voiceover.** Viewers cannot
  see thought ([Kouguell](https://scriptmag.com/columns/adapting-novels-memoirs-short-stories-keep-cut)).
  *Normal People* turned thoughts into small spoken changes and close, shallow-focus camera
  ([Creative Review](https://www.creativereview.co.uk/sally-rooney-normal-people-adaption/)).
- **Voiceover is used sparingly.** It fails when it repeats the picture or comes and goes; it works
  throughout or not at all ([John August](https://johnaugust.com/?p=150)), and best as counterpoint
  or irony (*Fight Club*, [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2013/07/jim-uhls-on-adapting-fight-club);
  *Gone Girl*'s deceiving diary, [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/gone-girl-voiceover-analysis)).
- **Point of view is rebalanced.** *Atonement* moved weight from Briony to Robbie and Cecilia
  ([PopEntertainment](https://popentertainmentarchives.com/post/christopher-hampton-adapting-atonement)).
- **Dialogue is compressed** to its essentials
  ([No Film School on the Coens](https://nofilmschool.com/coen-writing-tips-no-country-for-old-men)).
- **Endings may change** when a stronger screen ending exists; Gerwig built *Little Women*'s last
  act around Jo's publishing deal ([The Playlist](https://theplaylist.net/greta-gerwig-little-women-ending-clip-20191105/)).
- **Passive protagonists become active:** most events should happen because of the protagonist's
  choices ([Scriptangel](https://scriptangel.com/active-protagonists/)).

## What is expanded or added

- **Scenes the book only summarises.** Much of *Brokeback Mountain* was invented; one sentence of
  the story became a sequence ([AFI](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/54466-BROKEBACK-MOUNTAIN), secondhand).
- **Screen time for relationships the book implies.** Thompson invented scenes to keep Edward
  present when he is off the page ([Stovel](https://www.jasna.org/persuasions/on-line/vol32no1/stovel.html)).
- **New lines that remain traceable.** Gerwig says almost every line comes from the book or from
  research ([Scriptnotes 433](https://johnaugust.com/2020/scriptnotes-ep-433-the-one-with-greta-gerwig)).
- **Objects that carry inner change.** Nagy made Therese a photographer in *Carol*, because pictures
  show her growth ([Scene Creek](https://scenecreek.com/books-film-phyllis-nagy-carol/)).

## Fidelity

Keep the spirit, not every thread. Flynn aimed to keep *Gone Girl*'s tone, dark heart and odd
relationships rather than every plotline
([UPI](https://www.upi.com/Entertainment_News/Movies/2014/09/29/Gillian-Flynn-adapts-Gone-Girl-novel-for-the-big-screen/1811411941332/)).
Stephen King's test is to keep the story intact and make the changes the author wishes he had
thought of ([AP via Boston.com](https://www.boston.com/culture/books/2017/09/06/ap-exclusive-stephen-king-talks-it-and-other-adaptations/)).

## The decision, unit by unit

For each subplot, character, scene or passage:

1. **Spine.** Does it serve the central question, the theme, or the protagonist's outer or inner
   motivation? If none, cut it.
2. **Function.** Does something else already do this job? Merge or make a composite.
3. **Subplot.** Has it its own setup, turns and payoff, and does it both push and enrich the main line?
4. **Visibility.** Can it be shown? If it is inner, choose behaviour, an object, staging, dialogue
   with subtext, or counterpoint voiceover, in that order.
5. **Core and pitfalls.** What is this scene's core in one sentence, and what could go wrong?
6. **Agency.** Does it happen because of the protagonist, or only to them?
7. **Summary or scene.** Is something important summarised that should be a scene?
8. **Must survive.** Would the author grieve its loss?
9. **Redundancy.** Is the audience already seeing what this tells them?

## Relationship-led and inner-life-heavy fiction

- **The beloved needs a life of her own.** *The Price of Salt* filters Carol through her lover's
  eyes; for the film she had to be invented and given equal weight
  ([Scene Creek](https://scenecreek.com/books-film-phyllis-nagy-carol/)). A close-third novel on one
  lover leaves the other's public choices reading as cruelty unless she is given scenes.
- **Misunderstanding is the hardest thing to dramatise** without exposition
  ([Arts Foundation on *Normal People*](https://artsfoundation.co.uk/?p=6699)). Ambiguity is best
  kept in objects, ritual and sound, never explained.
- **Format is a real choice.** A limited series of short episodes keeps the time with the couple;
  a two-hour film forces heavy compression.
